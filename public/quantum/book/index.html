Week 1

The Deterministic Worldview and Why It Failed

18, 20 August · Objective 1

Classical physics is a total function: state in, future out, no exceptions. This week establishes that contract precisely enough that you can see what quantum mechanics breaks — because the break is narrower and stranger than the popular account suggests.

The classical contract

Newton's 1687 formulation makes a specific, testable promise. Given a system's complete state at time t — every position q and momentum p — the equations of motion return the state at any other time, past or future:

    F = ma          →     d²q/dt² = F(q)/m

Hamilton's 1833 reformulation makes the structure explicit. Define a scalar H(q, p) — the total energy — and the entire dynamics falls out of two symmetric equations:

    dq/dt =  ∂H/∂p
    dp/dt = -∂H/∂q

This is worth dwelling on as an architectural move. Hamilton replaced n coupled second-order equations with 2n first-order ones, and in doing so identified the true state space: phase space, where a point (q, p) is a complete system description. Every mechanical system, from a pendulum to the solar system, became a trajectory through phase space governed by one scalar function.

Analogy boundary — Hamiltonian as a reducer. H looks like a reducer: current state in, next state out, deterministic. The analogy holds for the type signature and fails on volume. Liouville's theorem says phase-space volume is conserved — classical evolution is measure-preserving and reversible. A software reducer freely collapses distinct inputs to one output. That distinction is not incidental; it is precisely what makes quantum gates unitary, and it is the reason you cannot build a quantum if that discards a branch.

What determinism actually claims

Laplace stated the strong form in 1814: an intellect knowing all positions and forces would find "nothing uncertain, and the future, as the past, would be present to its eyes."

Read as a systems claim, this asserts three separable properties, and it is worth keeping them apart because quantum mechanics does not treat them alike:

  1. Deterministic evolution. The state at t₁ fixes the state at t₂.
  2. Definite values. Every observable has a value at all times, measured or not.
  3. Non-disturbance. Observation reads state without altering it.

Quantum mechanics keeps (1) — the Schrödinger equation is as deterministic as Hamilton's. It abandons (2) and (3). This is the single most misreported fact about the theory. The wavefunction evolves with perfect predictability; what fails is the assumption that measurement is a passive read.

Analogy boundary — the read/write distinction. Classical (3) is the claim that observation is a pure read. Quantum measurement is closer to a compare-and-swap that always writes. But do not push this to "the observer's mind matters." Nothing in the formalism mentions consciousness. What matters is irreversible correlation with an environment of many degrees of freedom — which a photodiode accomplishes and a sleeping physicist does not.

Why it worked for three centuries

Classical mechanics was not a bad theory awaiting replacement. Le Verrier predicted Neptune's position from Uranus's orbital anomalies in 1846; Galle found it within one degree the same night. Boltzmann reduced thermodynamics to statistics with S = k ln W. Maxwell unified electricity, magnetism, and light into four equations by 1865.

The framing that serves you best: classical physics is a limit, not an error. It is the ħ → 0 boundary of a deeper theory, the way a synchronous single-threaded model is the zero-latency limit of a distributed system. Correct inside its regime, and silently wrong outside it — which is the more dangerous failure mode, because nothing signals the crossing.

The three cracks

By 1900 three experiments resisted the framework. Each gets a chapter, but note the shared shape now:

  CRACK                CLASSICAL PREDICTION        OBSERVED
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Blackbody            Energy → ∞ at short λ       Finite, peaked curve
    radiation          (ultraviolet catastrophe)
  Photoelectric        Brighter light → faster     Frequency sets energy;
    effect             electrons                   intensity sets count
  Atomic stability     Electron spirals into       Discrete spectral lines
                       nucleus in ~10⁻¹¹ s         Atoms are stable

All three fail the same way: a continuum theory meets a system that is stubbornly discrete. Energy comes in lumps. That is the whole of the quantum hypothesis, and every subsequent strangeness follows from taking it seriously.

Why i = √−1 becomes unavoidable

Classical physics uses complex numbers as bookkeeping — a convenience for oscillations, with Re(·) taken at the end. Quantum mechanics cannot do this. The state itself is complex-valued, and the phase is physical.

Here is the reason, and it is an information-theoretic one. A quantum state must carry two independent quantities per branch: a magnitude that governs probability, and a phase that governs interference between branches. Probabilities are non-negative reals; they cannot cancel. But quantum branches must be able to cancel — that is what the double-slit dark fringes are. So amplitudes must live in a field with negative and complex values, combining linearly, with magnitudes squared to yield probabilities:

    |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩       α, β ∈ ℂ      |α|² + |β|² = 1

Two complex numbers, four real parameters, minus normalization, minus an unobservable global phase — leaves two. That is the surface of a sphere, and it is why the Bloch sphere in Week 7 is a sphere and not something worse.

Analogy boundary — amplitude as a signed weight. Treating α as a signed weight captures cancellation and fails on rotation. A complex phase e^{iθ} is a continuous rotation in a two-dimensional plane, not a sign flip. Signs are the θ ∈ {0, π} special case; the gates in Week 7 use every angle between.

Try it yourself. Open bloch_sphere.html. Set the state to |0⟩ and apply H, then H again. You return to |0⟩. Now apply H, then Z, then H — you land on |1⟩. The Z did nothing observable on its own (it only changed a phase), yet it changed the final outcome completely. That is phase becoming physical through interference, and it is the mechanism every quantum algorithm exploits.

Key takeaways

  • Classical mechanics promises deterministic evolution, definite values, and non-disturbing observation. Quantum mechanics keeps the first and discards the other two.
  • Hamiltonian phase space identified state as (q, p) and made evolution volume-preserving — the classical ancestor of gate reversibility.
  • Classical physics is the ħ → 0 limit of quantum mechanics, not a refuted theory. It fails silently at the boundary.
  • All three cracks of 1900 are the same crack: continuum predictions against discrete reality.
  • Complex amplitudes are forced, not chosen. Interference requires cancellation; probabilities cannot cancel; amplitudes must.

Q1 (Objective 1) — Your audit pipeline is deterministic: same inputs, same hash chain. Someone claims this makes it "classical, not quantum." Using the three-part decomposition of determinism above, identify precisely which property your pipeline shares with classical mechanics and which property quantum mechanics also satisfies.

Q2 (Objective 1) — A colleague argues complex numbers in quantum mechanics are "just a math convenience, like phasors in AC circuit analysis." Give the specific physical observation that refutes this, and state what would have to be true of probability for the convenience view to hold.

Check your answers

A1 — Your pipeline shares deterministic evolution (property 1). Given identical inputs and identical prior chain state, it produces identical output — that is Hamiltonian evolution's defining feature, and quantum mechanics satisfies it too: the Schrödinger equation is fully deterministic in |ψ⟩.

The interesting half is where your pipeline resembles the quantum side. A hash-chained audit trail deliberately violates non-disturbance (property 3): writing an entry changes system state irreversibly, and there is no way to record an observation without advancing the chain. You have engineered, at the application layer, precisely the property quantum measurement has at the physical layer — observation that necessarily writes. The analogy is not that your pipeline is quantum; it is that you already have working intuition for a system where "read" is not free, which most people must acquire painfully.

Property 2, definite values, is where the pipeline stays classical: your records have determinate values before you read them.

A2 — The refutation is destructive interference: the dark fringes of the double-slit pattern. Regions that receive particles when either slit alone is open receive none when both are open. Two open paths yield less than one. Since probabilities are non-negative, P(both) ≥ P(one) always — no combination of non-negative numbers reproduces the observation. The quantities that combine must therefore support cancellation, which means they are not probabilities.

In phasor analysis the complex number is genuinely eliminable: take Re(·) at the end and nothing is lost, because the physical current is the real part. In quantum mechanics the elimination fails — you must square the magnitude after summing amplitudes, and |α + β|² ≠ |α|² + |β|². The cross term 2·Re(α*β) is the interference, and it carries the relative phase.

For the convenience view to hold, probability would have to admit negative values — which is to say it would not be probability. Attempts in this direction exist (Wigner quasi-probability distributions do go negative) and are informative precisely because that negativity is the signature of non-classicality.

Week 2

Cracks in Classical Physics

25, 27 August · Objectives 1, 2

Three experiments broke the continuum: blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, and double-slit interference with single particles. The first two force energy quantization. The third forces something harder — that a single indivisible object carries a distribution over paths, and that learning which path destroys it.

Blackbody radiation and the ultraviolet catastrophe

A blackbody absorbs all incident radiation and re-emits based only on temperature. Classical statistical mechanics assigns each electromagnetic mode in a cavity an average energy of kT (equipartition). The mode count grows as ν², so the predicted spectral density is:

    Rayleigh–Jeans:   ρ(ν) = (8πν²/c³) · kT        →  ∫ρ dν = ∞

The integral diverges. A warm cavity would radiate infinite energy, concentrated at short wavelengths. Ehrenfest named it the ultraviolet catastrophe in 1911. It is not a small discrepancy — it is a theory returning infinity where an oven returns a dull red glow.

Planck's 1900 fix: permit mode energies only in integer multiples of hν.

    Planck:   ρ(ν) = (8πν²/c³) · hν/(e^{hν/kT} − 1)

The exponential suppresses high-frequency modes. When hν ≫ kT, thermal energy cannot pay the entry cost of even one quantum, and the mode stays silent. This is a minimum transaction size, and it is the entire quantum hypothesis.

Planck regarded it as a mathematical trick for six years and disliked it. The constant that fell out — h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s — is now the definition of the kilogram.

Analogy boundary — quantization as discretization. Energy quantization resembles fixed-point arithmetic: values on a lattice, not a continuum. It fails on two counts. First, the spacing hν is not universal — it scales with frequency, so different modes have different grid sizes. Second, and more importantly, a quantum system can occupy a superposition of lattice points, which no fixed-point register can. Quantization constrains measurement outcomes, not the state.

The photoelectric effect

Light on metal ejects electrons. Classically, energy arrives continuously, so brighter light should eject faster electrons, and dim light should work after a delay while energy accumulates.

Observed instead:

  OBSERVATION                          CLASSICAL EXPECTATION
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Below threshold ν₀: nothing,         Any frequency works given
    at any intensity                     enough intensity
  Above ν₀: KE depends on ν only       KE rises with intensity
  Intensity sets electron COUNT        Intensity sets electron ENERGY
  Emission is immediate (< 10⁻⁹ s)     Dim light needs seconds to build

Einstein's 1905 account — the work that earned his Nobel, not relativity — treats light as quanta of energy hν:

    KE_max = hν − φ          φ = work function of the metal

The logic is per-photon. One photon either clears the work function or does not. A brighter beam of insufficient photons is more failed attempts, not one success.

Analogy boundary — photons as packets. The packet framing gets threshold behavior exactly right: a message under the minimum size is rejected regardless of send rate. It fails on identity and locality. Photons are excitations of a field mode, not localized objects in flight — they have no trajectory between emission and absorption, and they are strictly indistinguishable in a way no packet with a sequence number is. Push the packet analogy into the double-slit and it produces the wrong answer, as the next section shows.

The double-slit experiment

Young established the wave nature of light in 1801 by passing it through two slits and observing fringes. Davisson and Germer accidentally did the same for electrons in 1927, confirming de Broglie's 1924 hypothesis that matter has wavelength λ = h/p.

The version that matters fires particles one at a time:

        SOURCE        BARRIER          SCREEN
                      ┌──────┐         ░░▓▓░░▓▓░░
          ·   ──────► │  ══  │  ─────► ░▓▓██▓▓██▓░
       (one           │      │         ░░▓▓░░▓▓░░
        electron)     │  ══  │         ▒▒░░▒▒░░▒▒
                      └──────┘
                                       fringes emerge over
                                       thousands of single hits

Each electron makes one localized dot. The dots accumulate into interference fringes. The electron is indivisible — it never splits — yet the pattern requires contributions from both slits. A single electron's amplitude passes through both.

Now the part that makes this a quantum-information result. Place a detector at one slit to record the path. The fringes vanish, replaced by two classical bands. And this holds regardless of detector mechanism — the disturbance can be made arbitrarily gentle. What destroys interference is not momentum kick but the existence of which-path information anywhere in the universe.

Formally: interference requires the two paths to be indistinguishable. If path information is imprinted on any system, the branches no longer share a final state and cannot interfere:

    Interference:      (|L⟩ + |R⟩)/√2                    → fringes
    Path recorded:     (|L⟩|d_L⟩ + |R⟩|d_R⟩)/√2          → no fringes,
                                                            because ⟨d_L|d_R⟩ = 0

The fringes return if you erase the detector record before it becomes permanent — the quantum eraser. Interference tracks information availability, not physical roughness.

Analogy boundary — which-path as an audit record. This is the strongest analogy in the course and worth stating precisely. Writing a which-path record is exactly like appending to an audit trail: it makes one branch of a formerly-open computation permanently distinguishable and forecloses the alternative. The boundary: your audit entry records a fact that was already true. The which-path detector does not record a pre-existing path — before entanglement with the detector, the electron had no path. The record creates the fact it appears to log. That has no classical counterpart, and it is why "hidden variables" is a substantive claim rather than a truism.

Try it yourself. Open double_slit.html. Run in particle mode and watch the fringes build from individual hits — note that the first hundred look random. Then enable the which-path detector and watch the pattern collapse to two bands. The transition is not gradual in detector strength; it tracks how much information the detector actually acquires.

Bohr's atom

Rutherford's 1911 nuclear atom is classically unstable: an orbiting electron accelerates, radiates, and spirals in within ~10⁻¹¹ seconds. Matter should not exist.

Bohr's 1913 postulate: angular momentum is quantized, L = nħ. Electrons occupy stationary states and radiate only when transitioning between them.

    E_n = −13.6 eV / n²          ΔE = E_final − E_initial = hν

This predicts hydrogen's spectral lines to four significant figures — the Balmer series falls out exactly. It is also, as Bohr knew, internally incoherent: it grafts a quantization rule onto classical orbits without justifying either. It fails for helium.

De Broglie supplied the missing why in 1924. If electrons have wavelength λ = h/p, a stationary orbit is one holding a standing wave — an integer number of wavelengths around the circumference:

    nλ = 2πr    with λ = h/p    ⟹    L = pr = nħ

Bohr's postulate is a boundary condition on a wave. This is the last step before Schrödinger replaces orbits with orbitals in Week 3.

Key takeaways

  • Blackbody radiation forces a minimum energy transaction of hν per mode; the exponential suppression of costly modes resolves the divergence.
  • The photoelectric effect is per-photon logic: threshold frequency, intensity setting count rather than energy, and instant emission.
  • Single-particle double-slit interference shows one indivisible object carrying amplitude over multiple paths.
  • Which-path information destroys interference independent of mechanism. Availability of information, not disturbance, is the operative variable.
  • Bohr's quantized orbits reproduce hydrogen's spectrum; de Broglie explains them as standing waves, converting an ad hoc rule into a boundary condition.

Check your answers

A1 — Nothing about the past changes, and the resolution lies in what the screen data alone contains.

The full set of hits shows no interference — it is a smooth blob, both when path information is kept and when it is later erased. Fringes appear only in subsets of the data, selected by the detector's outcome. Erasing which-path information does not restore fringes to the existing pattern; it makes available a sorting key that partitions the hits into two subensembles, each of which shows fringes, with the two sets phase-shifted so their sum is the featureless blob you already had.

The audit framing that makes this click: you are not rewriting log entries. You are gaining the ability to GROUP BY a column that was always in the data. The correlations existed at hit time; what the erasure changes is your access to the key. This is exactly why no signal can be sent — the marginal distribution at the screen is fixed the instant the particle lands, and a distant experimenter's choice cannot alter it. Retrocausality would require the marginal to change; it does not.

A2 — It is a model fit to one instance rather than derived from an interface — a lookup table with a plausible-looking closed form. Bohr had a correct constraint (quantization) attached to an incorrect mechanism (classical orbits), tuned against the one system whose spectrum he had: hydrogen, the single-electron case where electron–electron interaction is absent and the error is invisible.

The four-significant-figure agreement is the danger. A model that is obviously wrong is discarded cheaply. A model that is precisely right in the tested regime and structurally wrong outside it acquires institutional weight — it gets taught, extended, and defended. The failure at helium was the load test that revealed the fit had no mechanism behind it.

The recognizable pattern: a component passing every integration test because the suite covers one code path. Bohr, to his credit, said so himself — he called the model provisional from the start and spent a decade seeking the theory beneath it. The proper fix was not patching the orbits but replacing the abstraction, which is what Schrödinger and Heisenberg did in 1925–26.

Week 3

The Birth of Quantum Mechanics

1, 3 September · Objectives 1, 2

In eighteen months, 1925–1926, two incompatible-looking formalisms appeared and were proven equivalent. What emerged was a theory with a deterministic evolution law, a probabilistic measurement rule, and no agreed account of how the second relates to the first. That gap is the measurement problem, and it is still open.

Two formalisms

Heisenberg, 1925. Working from the position that a theory should reference only observable quantities — spectral frequencies and intensities, not unobservable orbits — Heisenberg built an algebra of transition amplitudes. Born recognized the arrays as matrices. The defining result is that they do not commute:

    [x̂, p̂] = x̂p̂ − p̂x̂ = iħ

Schrödinger, 1926. Following de Broglie's standing waves, Schrödinger wrote an equation for a continuous wavefunction ψ(x, t):

    iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ           Ĥ = −(ħ²/2m)∇² + V(x)

Schrödinger proved the two equivalent in 1926: same predictions, different bases. Dirac and von Neumann then showed both are representations of vectors and operators on Hilbert space.

The systems reading: Heisenberg wrote the operator interface — what you can do to a state. Schrödinger wrote the state representation — what a state is. Neither is more fundamental; they are the same abstraction viewed from opposite sides, which is why Dirac notation, designed to be basis-agnostic, superseded both.

Non-commutation and uncertainty

[x̂, p̂] = iħ is the theory's structural core. Two observables whose operators fail to commute have no shared eigenbasis — no state assigns both a definite value.

    σ_x σ_p ≥ ħ/2

This is not measurement clumsiness. It is a statement about the state itself: a state sharply peaked in position is necessarily a broad superposition in momentum, because position and momentum bases are Fourier transforms of one another. The bound is a property of Fourier pairs, and it holds before anyone measures anything.

Analogy boundary — non-commuting operations. Order-dependence is familiar: rotations in 3-D don't commute, and neither do two writes to shared state. The analogy captures order-dependence and misses the deeper claim. Non-commuting matrix operations still act on a state with definite entries. Quantum non-commutation says there is no assignment of simultaneous values at all. Bell's theorem in Week 4 makes this precise and testable — which is what elevates it above interpretive preference.

The Born rule

Schrödinger initially read |ψ|² as physical charge density — the electron literally smeared out. This fails: wavepackets spread, and detectors always fire at points.

Born's 1926 correction, delivered in a footnote:

    P(outcome m) = |⟨m|ψ⟩|²

|ψ|² is a probability density. The state does not tell you what will happen; it gives you a distribution over what can.

This creates the theory's central asymmetry — two evolution rules that do not resemble each other:

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ UNITARY EVOLUTION (Schrödinger)                                  │
  │   Deterministic · Linear · Reversible · Continuous               │
  │   |ψ(t)⟩ = U(t)|ψ(0)⟩,  U†U = I                                  │
  ├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ MEASUREMENT (Born + projection)                                  │
  │   Probabilistic · Non-linear · Irreversible · Discontinuous      │
  │   |ψ⟩ → |m⟩ with probability |⟨m|ψ⟩|²                            │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Every interpretive dispute in quantum mechanics is an argument about the relationship between those two boxes.

Analogy boundary — Born rule as a typed outcome envelope. Reading |ψ⟩ as Distribution<Outcome> with weights is a good working model: the type carries possible results and their probabilities. The boundary is that a probability distribution is basis-independent — a die's outcome distribution does not change because you ask about it differently. |ψ⟩ yields a different distribution in every measurement basis, and those distributions are not marginals of any joint distribution. That is exactly what Bell's theorem rules out.

The measurement problem

State it as a contradiction between three individually plausible claims:

  1. The wavefunction is a complete description of a physical system.
  2. The wavefunction always evolves unitarily (linearly, deterministically).
  3. Measurements have single definite outcomes.

Take a qubit in (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and a measuring device D. Linearity gives:

    (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 ⊗ |D_ready⟩  →  (|0⟩|D_0⟩ + |1⟩|D_1⟩)/√2

The device ends in superposition. Nothing in unitary evolution selects a branch. Yet the pointer reads one value. The three claims cannot all be true, and each major interpretation denies a different one:

InterpretationDeniesCost
Copenhagen(1) — ψ is knowledge, not a full descriptionWhere is the classical/quantum cut?
Many-worlds(3) — all outcomes occur, in branchesWhy does the Born rule hold?
Bohmian mechanics(1) — particles have definite positions tooExplicit non-locality
Objective collapse (GRW)(2) — collapse is a real physical processNew physics; testable, untested

Decoherence — Zurek and Zeh, 1970s onward — explains why we never see superposed pointers. A macroscopic device entangles with ~10²³ environmental degrees of freedom in femtoseconds, and interference between branches becomes unobservable in practice. This is solid, calculable physics and it dissolves the appearance problem. It does not solve the measurement problem: it explains why branches stop interfering, not why one is selected. Conflating the two is the most common error in the literature you will encounter.

Analogy boundary — decoherence as information leakage. Decoherence is genuinely information leakage: which-path data escapes into the environment, which is why it is irreversible in the same statistical sense as your audit chain — reversal is not forbidden, only overwhelmingly improbable. The boundary: leakage explains loss of interference, never outcome selection. In your pipeline, a state machine that stops being able to backtrack still ends in one state because it was always in one state. A decohered quantum system has no such prior fact — that is precisely what remains unexplained.

The Copenhagen interpretation

Bohr and Heisenberg's position, roughly: the formalism predicts measurement outcomes, and asking what happens between measurements is not a well-posed question. Bohr's complementarity holds that wave and particle descriptions are both necessary and mutually exclusive; which applies depends on the experimental arrangement, and no single picture covers both.

Einstein objected for two decades — not to the predictions, which he never disputed, but to the claim of completeness. "God does not play dice" is remembered; the substantive objection came in 1935 with EPR, and is Week 4's material.

The engineering assessment: Copenhagen is an interface specification with the implementation deliberately unspecified. That is defensible for a working programmer and unsatisfying for an architect, which is roughly how the field has been divided ever since.

Key takeaways

  • Matrix mechanics and wave mechanics are the same theory in different bases; Dirac notation is the basis-agnostic form.
  • [x̂, p̂] = iħ implies no shared eigenbasis for position and momentum. Uncertainty is a property of states, not of instruments.
  • The Born rule makes |ψ|² a probability density, splitting the theory into deterministic unitary evolution and probabilistic measurement.
  • The measurement problem is the inconsistency of completeness, universal unitarity, and definite outcomes. Interpretations differ in which they abandon.
  • Decoherence explains the absence of visible macroscopic superposition. It does not explain outcome selection, and the distinction is load-bearing.

Check your answers

A1 — The missing step is outcome selection. Decoherence shows the reduced density matrix of the system becomes diagonal in the pointer basis — interference terms are suppressed below any observable threshold. This is a rigorous result and explains why you never see a superposed pointer.

But a diagonal density matrix describes an ensemble of possible outcomes with classical probabilities. It does not describe one outcome occurring. Formally, decoherence takes you from a coherent superposition to an improper mixture; the transition from improper mixture to a single actualized result is not derived — it is assumed. Everett's response is to deny that any selection happens; Copenhagen's is to say the question is malformed. Neither is decoherence, which is a mechanism, not an interpretation, and is common to all of them.

The consensus analogy: a protocol that guarantees all nodes converge on one value has solved agreement, not ordering. Every node reports the same answer, and no node can reconstruct which proposal arrived first — the information is genuinely destroyed. Decoherence guarantees the branches stop talking to each other. It says nothing about which branch you find yourself in, and there is no fact in the formalism to appeal to.

A2 — The disturbance reading says each observable has a definite value, and the apparatus perturbs it — an epistemic limit on a system with well-defined properties. Non-commutation says something ontologically stronger: no state assigns definite values to both simultaneously, no matter how gently you probe. There is nothing to disturb.

The distinguishing experiments are Bell tests (Week 4). If particles carried definite pre-existing values for all measurable quantities, correlations across separated measurements would satisfy Bell's inequality regardless of disturbance — the bound follows from the mere existence of a joint distribution over outcomes, not from any assumption about apparatus. Experiments violate the inequality, and the loophole-free tests of 2015 (Delft, NIST, Vienna) closed the locality and detection gaps simultaneously. The 2022 Nobel to Aspect, Clauser, and Zeilinger recognized this line of work.

The refined statement: quantum mechanics does not merely limit what you can learn about simultaneous values. It denies that the values exist to be learned.

Week 4

Superposition, Entanglement, and Bell's Theorem

8, 10 September · Objective 2

Superposition is the resource; entanglement is the correlation that has no classical explanation. Bell's theorem converts a philosophical dispute into an experimental one, and the experiment has been run. This is the week where "quantum is weird" becomes a measured number.

Superposition, precisely

A qubit state is a normalized complex combination of basis states:

    |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩          |α|² + |β|² = 1

Three clarifications that prevent most downstream errors:

It is not "both 0 and 1." It is a definite vector in a two-dimensional complex space that happens not to be an eigenvector of the measurement operator you chose. In the basis {|+⟩, |−⟩} where |±⟩ = (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2, the state |+⟩ is not superposed — it is a basis state with a certain outcome. Superposition is basis-relative. Entanglement is not.

It is not ignorance. A coin under a cup is 50/50 because you lack information; the coin has a value. Measuring |+⟩ in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis gives 50/50, but measuring in {|+⟩, |−⟩} gives |+⟩ with certainty. No probability distribution over hidden values reproduces both.

Global phase is unobservable; relative phase is everything. e^{iγ}|ψ⟩ is physically identical to |ψ⟩. But (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 are perfectly distinguishable — apply H to each and measure.

Analogy boundary — superposition as pending dispatch. An agent holding several candidate execution paths before commit is a good first model: multiple outcomes live, weights attached, one selected at dispatch. Two failures. First, your candidate set is basis-independent — the paths are the paths regardless of how you enumerate them; quantum "paths" are an artifact of basis choice. Second, and fatally, your weights are probabilities that only ever add. Amplitudes cancel. An agent whose two live paths can annihilate on merge has no software analogue, and that cancellation is where every quantum speedup comes from.

Entanglement

For two qubits, the state space is the tensor product — four dimensions, spanned by |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. A state is separable if it factors:

    |ψ⟩ = |a⟩ ⊗ |b⟩

and entangled otherwise. The canonical example is a Bell state:

    |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2

No |a⟩, |b⟩ produce this. Try it: expanding (a₀|0⟩ + a₁|1⟩)(b₀|0⟩ + b₁|1⟩) requires a₀b₁ = 0 and a₁b₀ = 0 while a₀b₀ and a₁b₁ are both nonzero — inconsistent.

The consequence: each qubit individually is in a maximally mixed state, carrying no information. All the information lives in the correlation. Measure the first in any basis and the second is instantly determined in that same basis, at any separation.

Analogy boundary — entanglement as shared state. Reading a Bell pair as two references to one object captures the correlation and gets the mechanism backwards in a way that matters. Shared mutable state implies a channel — writes propagate, and you could signal. Entanglement cannot transmit information: the marginal distribution at each end is uniform regardless of what the other party does, which is the no-signaling theorem. The correlation is only visible when the two records are brought together and compared — which requires a classical channel at light speed. Closer to your domain: two nodes deterministically deriving the same value from a shared seed. That analogy is too good, and Bell's theorem is precisely the proof that no shared seed exists.

EPR and the completeness argument

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, 1935. Take |Φ⁺⟩ with the halves separated. Measure qubit A in the Z basis and you know B's Z value with certainty, without touching B. Measure A in X instead and you know B's X value with certainty.

Their reasoning: if you can predict a quantity with certainty without disturbing the system, that quantity corresponds to an "element of reality." Since you could have chosen either basis, and the choice at A cannot affect B (locality), B must possess definite values for both Z and X. But quantum mechanics assigns no such state. Therefore the description is incomplete — there are hidden variables.

This is a rigorous argument. Its premises are locality and realism, and its conclusion follows. Bohr's reply was, by wide agreement, obscure. For thirty years the dispute was considered untestable metaphysics.

Bell's theorem

Bell's 1964 insight: the hidden-variable hypothesis has testable consequences, and they differ from quantum mechanics.

Assume each particle carries variables λ fixing outcomes for every measurement setting, and that A's setting cannot influence B's outcome. Measure spin along directions a, a′ (at A) and b, b′ (at B), outcomes ±1. Define the correlation E(a, b) = ⟨A·B⟩. Then the CHSH combination obeys:

    S = |E(a,b) − E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) + E(a′,b′)|  ≤  2

The derivation needs nothing about quantum mechanics — only that a joint distribution over all four outcomes exists. It is essentially a counting argument.

Quantum mechanics predicts, for |Φ⁺⟩ at optimal angles (45° apart):

    S = 2√2 ≈ 2.828        (Tsirelson's bound)

Experiment agrees with quantum mechanics. Aspect closed the timing loophole in 1982; the 2015 loophole-free tests at Delft, NIST, and Vienna closed locality and detection together. Nobel Prize, 2022.

What this rules out: any theory that is both local and realist. Something has to go. Most physicists drop realism (no pre-existing values); Bohmians drop locality. Nothing forces the choice, but the conjunction is dead.

Analogy boundary — the shared-seed model. Two nodes with a common seed produce identical outputs with no communication and perfectly mimic entanglement for one fixed measurement setting. Bell's theorem is exactly the statement that no seed reproduces the correlations across all setting pairs. If you could write a deterministic function f(λ, setting) → ±1 matching quantum predictions, you would violate a counting bound. This is the single sharpest place where a systems intuition must be surrendered rather than refined.

Key takeaways

  • Superposition is basis-relative: every state is a basis state in some basis. Entanglement is basis-independent and cannot be transformed away.
  • Amplitudes cancel; probabilities do not. That difference is the source of quantum advantage.
  • Entangled subsystems carry no individual information; the information is in the correlation. No-signaling holds — a classical channel is still required.
  • EPR is a valid argument from locality plus realism to incompleteness. Bell showed those premises are experimentally refutable.
  • CHSH: local realism gives S ≤ 2; quantum mechanics gives 2√2. Experiment confirms 2√2, loophole-free since 2015.

Week 5

Tunneling, and What Makes Quantum Information Different

15, 17 September · Objective 2

Tunneling is the clearest case of a quantum effect with unambiguous engineering consequences — it powers flash memory, scanning tunneling microscopes, and stellar fusion, and it sets a hard floor on transistor scaling. Then the shift from physics to information: what a qubit is as a resource, and why no-cloning is the theorem your entire professional intuition should recognize.

Tunneling

A particle with energy E meets a barrier of height V > E. Classically the region is forbidden — kinetic energy would be negative. Quantum mechanically, ψ does not stop at the wall; it decays exponentially inside it, and if the barrier is thin, a nonzero amplitude emerges:

    E ──────┐  ┌─── E                Inside barrier:
            │  │                       ψ(x) ∝ e^{−κx}
      ψ ~~~~│▓▓│~~~~ ψ (attenuated)
            │▓▓│                     κ = √(2m(V−E))/ħ
            └──┘
             ← d →                   T ≈ e^{−2κd}

The transmission probability is exponential in barrier width and in √m. Two consequences worth internalizing:

  • Exponential sensitivity. An STM exploits this: tunneling current changes by ~an order of magnitude per ångström of tip height, which is why it resolves individual atoms.
  • Mass dependence. Electrons tunnel readily; protons rarely; anything macroscopic never. The √m in κ is why this is a microscopic phenomenon.

Applications: flash memory writes by forcing electrons through an oxide barrier into a floating gate. Alpha decay is tunneling out of a nuclear potential — Gamow's 1928 model explained the Geiger–Nuttall relation, spanning 20 orders of magnitude in half-life. Stellar fusion requires tunneling through Coulomb repulsion; without it, the Sun does not burn.

And the constraint: below roughly 5 nm of gate oxide, leakage current from tunneling becomes unmanageable. This is a principal reason Dennard scaling ended — a quantum effect setting the economics of the semiconductor industry.

Analogy boundary — tunneling as a rare event. Treating it as a low-probability path through a rejected branch gets the statistics right and the mechanism wrong. Your rare event is a real trajectory taken infrequently. There is no trajectory through the barrier — the particle is never in the forbidden region with negative kinetic energy. Asking "how long did it take to cross" has no settled answer (the tunneling-time problem is still live). The transmitted amplitude is a boundary-matching result, not a path.

Bits and qubits

The comparison, stated as an interface:

  CLASSICAL BIT                      QUBIT
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  State: b ∈ {0, 1}                  State: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, α,β ∈ ℂ
  2 states                           Continuum (surface of a sphere)
  Read: returns b, non-destructive    Read: returns 0 or 1 probabilistically,
                                        destroys the superposition
  Copy: trivial (fan-out)            Copy: impossible (no-cloning)
  n bits: n values                   n qubits: 2ⁿ amplitudes
  Operations: any function           Operations: unitary only (reversible)

The row that misleads: n qubits require 2ⁿ complex amplitudes to describe, and this is routinely reported as "exponential parallel computation." It is not. You can only read out n bits. Measurement collapses the 2ⁿ amplitudes to one n-bit string. The exponential description is real; exponential access is not.

Quantum advantage therefore never comes from parallelism alone. It comes from arranging interference so that wrong answers cancel and right answers reinforce before measurement. Every algorithm in Weeks 11–15 is an instance of that pattern, and it is why quantum computers are not general-purpose accelerators.

Analogy boundary — the qubit as a probabilistic bit. A qubit is not a biased coin. A biased coin is described by one real number p ∈ [0,1]; a qubit needs two real parameters, and the extra one — relative phase — is invisible to any single measurement yet determines the result of measurements in other bases. Model a qubit as a random bit and every interference effect disappears from your predictions, which means every quantum algorithm becomes inexplicable.

Reversibility

Unitary operators satisfy U†U = I, so every quantum gate is invertible. This is not a design choice; it follows from the Schrödinger equation, and it has a physical basis.

Landauer's principle, 1961: erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 of energy. Erasure is the only fundamentally irreversible operation, and it costs. Bennett showed in 1973 that any computation can be made reversible by keeping enough intermediate state.

Practical consequence: classical AND is irreversible — output 0 does not determine the inputs. So there is no two-qubit "AND gate." The quantum version is Toffoli (CCNOT), which is three-qubit and reversible: it flips the target if both controls are 1, keeping the controls. Reversible computation must carry its garbage, and managing that is a real cost in circuit design.

Analogy boundary — reversibility as an append-only log. Your hash chain is append-only, and reconstructing prior state from the log is a genuine analogue of unitary invertibility. The boundary is where the information sits. Your log grows — reversibility is purchased with storage. A unitary keeps the state space fixed and rearranges it; no records accumulate. Quantum computation is reversible without a log, which is why uncomputation (running a subroutine backwards to clear ancillas) is mandatory rather than optional.

The no-cloning theorem

Claim. No unitary U satisfies U|ψ⟩|0⟩ = |ψ⟩|ψ⟩ for all |ψ⟩.

Proof. Suppose it did, for |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩. Unitaries preserve inner products:

    ⟨ψ|φ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩ · ⟨ψ|φ⟩   ⟹   z = z²   ⟹   z ∈ {0, 1}

So cloning works only for states that are identical or orthogonal — that is, for a pre-agreed basis, which is classical copying. Arbitrary unknown states cannot be duplicated. The proof is four lines and follows from linearity alone. ∎

Consequences:

  • No quantum backups. You cannot snapshot a register mid-computation.
  • No copy-based error correction. Classical repetition codes are unavailable; quantum error correction (Week 16) spreads one logical qubit across many physical ones without ever copying the state.
  • Quantum key distribution is secure by physics. An eavesdropper cannot copy the transmitted qubits and must measure, which disturbs them detectably. BB84 rests entirely on this.

Analogy boundary — no-cloning as tamper evidence. This is the strongest analogy available to you, and it is nearly exact in its consequences: an unknown quantum state cannot be duplicated without detection, exactly as a hash-chained record cannot be altered without breaking the chain. Both make interception evident rather than impossible. The boundary is the source of the guarantee. Your chain is secure because inverting SHA-256 is computationally infeasible — a sufficiently powerful adversary, or a broken hash, defeats it. No-cloning is information-theoretic: it holds against unlimited computation, forever, because it follows from linearity. That distinction is the entire argument for QKD over classical key exchange, and it is worth being precise about in any threat model you write.

Key takeaways

  • Tunneling probability falls exponentially with barrier width and √m — enabling STM and flash memory, and setting the leakage floor that ended Dennard scaling.
  • n qubits carry 2ⁿ amplitudes but yield only n bits on measurement. Exponential description, linear readout.
  • Quantum advantage comes from engineered interference, not parallelism.
  • All quantum gates are unitary and reversible; irreversible classical gates like AND require reversible embeddings such as Toffoli.
  • No-cloning follows from linearity in four lines. It forbids quantum backups and repetition codes, and it makes QKD information-theoretically secure.

Dirac Notation and State Vectors

22, 24 September · Objective 3 · Quiz 1 this week

Dirac notation is an interface specification. It separates what a state is from how it is represented, so that operations can be written once and evaluated in whatever basis is convenient. Treat it as typed notation and the algebra becomes mechanical.

Kets, bras, and types

A ket |ψ⟩ is a vector in a complex Hilbert space — for one qubit, a column in ℂ²:

    |0⟩ = [1]      |1⟩ = [0]      |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = [α]
          [0]            [1]                          [β]

A bra ⟨φ| is the dual — the conjugate transpose, a row vector:

    ⟨φ| = |φ⟩† = [φ₀*  φ₁*]

The value of the notation is that the type of every expression is readable from its shape:

Analogy boundary — bra/ket as a typed contract. Kets are the state type, bras the measurement-side consumer, and ⟨φ|ψ⟩ the only well-typed pairing that returns a scalar. The analogy is genuinely load-bearing: it prevents the most common algebra errors, because ill-typed expressions are visibly ill-shaped. Where it breaks: your interfaces are usually covariant in one direction, while the bra–ket relationship is antilinear — ⟨φ| for the ket c|φ⟩ is c*⟨φ|, with the conjugate. Lose the conjugate and probabilities stop being real.

The inner product and the Born rule

    ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = φ₀*ψ₀ + φ₁*ψ₁       ∈ ℂ

Properties: ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩*, linear in the ket, antilinear in the bra, and ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1 for normalized states.

The Born rule in this notation is a single line:

    P(outcome φ) = |⟨φ|ψ⟩|²

Orthogonal states (⟨φ|ψ⟩ = 0) are perfectly distinguishable; identical states have overlap 1. Everything between is the degree to which one measurement can confuse two states — which is exactly what no-cloning turned on last week.

Operators, Hermiticity, and unitarity

Two operator classes do all the work:

Hermitian (Â† = Â) represent observables. Real eigenvalues (measurement outcomes) and orthogonal eigenvectors (distinguishable results). The Pauli operators:

    X = [0 1]    Y = [0 −i]    Z = [1  0]    I = [1 0]
        [1 0]        [i  0]        [0 −1]        [0 1]

All Hermitian, all with eigenvalues ±1. Z's eigenvectors are |0⟩, |1⟩; X's are |±⟩.

Unitary (Û†Û = I) represent evolution. Norm-preserving, invertible, and — as established in Week 5 — the only operations quantum mechanics permits between measurements.

The Paulis are both Hermitian and unitary (since X² = Y² = Z² = I), which is why they serve as observables and as gates. That coincidence is specific to involutory operators; the Hadamard shares it, most gates do not.

The spectral decomposition connects the two roles:

    Â = Σₘ aₘ |m⟩⟨m|

An observable is its eigenvalues weighted by projectors onto its eigenstates. Measurement is the application of one such projector, selected stochastically by the Born rule. Z = |0⟩⟨0| − |1⟩⟨1| is worth verifying by hand.

The mean outcome over many measurements on identically prepared states. Note that this is never what a single run gives you — quantum hardware returns samples, and every observable in Weeks 9–15 is estimated from repeated shots. Budgeting shots is a real experimental cost: statistical error falls as 1/√N.

Composite systems

Two qubits combine by tensor product:

    |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ = |ψφ⟩,   written |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩

Dimension multiplies: n qubits span 2ⁿ dimensions. Operators compose the same way — X ⊗ I applies X to qubit 0 and leaves qubit 1 alone.

Analogy boundary — tensor product as a struct. Combining subsystems into a composite resembles a product type, and for separable states the analogy holds exactly. It fails for entangled states, which is the whole point: a struct is always the pair of its fields, and you can project out either one losslessly. An entangled state has no well-defined field values — the partial trace yields a mixed state, not a pure component. Product types describe only the separable corner of the space, which is a vanishing fraction of it.

Conjugate symmetry plus linearity in one argument forces antilinearity in the other. There is no choice available — it is the only structure on ℂⁿ supporting a probabilistic reading, which is why the same conjugation appears in the dagger of every operator.

The Bloch Sphere and Single-Qubit Gates

29 September, 1 October · Objective 3

Two complex amplitudes, four real parameters, minus normalization, minus global phase, leaves two — and two parameters is a sphere. The Bloch sphere is not a visualization aid bolted on afterward; it is the exact state space of one qubit, and every single-qubit gate is a rotation of it.

Constructing the sphere

Start from |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ and remove what is not physical. Write α = r₀e^{iγ₀}, β = r₁e^{iγ₁}. Global phase is unobservable, so factor out e^{iγ₀}:

    |ψ⟩ ≃ r₀|0⟩ + r₁e^{i(γ₁−γ₀)}|1⟩

Only the relative phase φ = γ₁ − γ₀ survives. Normalization r₀² + r₁² = 1 lets you write r₀ = cos(θ/2), r₁ = sin(θ/2). The general state is:

    |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩

Pauli gates

    X = [0 1]     Y = [0 −i]     Z = [1  0]
        [1 0]         [i  0]         [0 −1]

Each is a π rotation about its axis.

X — bit flip, the quantum NOT. X|0⟩ = |1⟩, X|1⟩ = |0⟩. Rotation by π about x̂. Note it leaves |+⟩ and |−⟩ fixed (they lie on the rotation axis).

Z — phase flip. Z|0⟩ = |0⟩, Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩. Rotation by π about ẑ. It leaves |0⟩ and |1⟩ alone up to phase, and swaps |+⟩ ↔ |−⟩. Z has no classical analogue — it does nothing observable in the computational basis, yet it is essential, because interference converts phase into amplitude.

Y — both, with a phase: Y = iXZ.

All satisfy P² = I: applying twice returns the identity, as a π rotation twice is a 2π rotation.

Hadamard

    H = (1/√2) [1  1]        H|0⟩ = |+⟩       H|+⟩ = |0⟩
               [1 −1]        H|1⟩ = |−⟩       H|−⟩ = |1⟩

H is a π rotation about the diagonal axis (x̂+ẑ)/√2, which exchanges the x and z axes. It maps the computational basis to the superposition basis and back, and H² = I.

This is the most-used gate in the course: it is how you create superposition from a classical input, and — applied again at the end — how you convert phase differences back into measurable amplitude differences. That sandwich, H → phase manipulation → H, is the skeleton of Deutsch's algorithm (Week 11) and of Grover's diffusion operator (Week 13).

Phase gates

    S = [1 0]  = √Z          T = [1 0     ]  = √S
        [0 i]                    [0 e^{iπ/4}]

S rotates π/2 about ẑ; T rotates π/4. Both act only on the |1⟩ component's phase.

T matters disproportionately. The Clifford group — generated by H, S, and CNOT — is efficiently simulable classically (Gottesman–Knill theorem). Adding T makes the set universal. So T gates are the source of quantum advantage, and in fault-tolerant architectures they are also by far the most expensive to implement, requiring magic-state distillation. "T-count" is a primary cost metric in quantum compilation — the number to watch when you read resource estimates in Week 16.

Rotation gates

For continuous control, the parameterized rotations:

    Rx(θ) = e^{−iθX/2} = [ cos(θ/2)    −i sin(θ/2)]
                         [−i sin(θ/2)   cos(θ/2) ]

    Ry(θ) = [cos(θ/2)  −sin(θ/2)]      Rz(θ) = [e^{−iθ/2}      0    ]
            [sin(θ/2)   cos(θ/2)]              [    0      e^{iθ/2} ]

The half-angles again: Rx(2π) = −I, not I. A qubit requires a 4π rotation to return exactly — spinor behavior, and a real physical effect confirmed by neutron interferometry.

Any single-qubit unitary decomposes as three rotations (up to global phase):

    U = Rz(γ) Ry(β) Rz(α)

This is what a transpiler does when it maps your circuit onto a device's native gate set.

Try it yourself. In bloch_sphere.html, verify these by hand:

  1. H then Z then H — equals X. Watch the axis exchange do the work.
  2. T eight times — returns to start (T⁸ = I), since 8 × π/4 = 2π.
  3. X on |+⟩ — nothing moves. The state is on the rotation axis.
  4. S then S — equals Z. Confirms S = √Z.

Key takeaways

  • One qubit's state space is exactly the Bloch sphere surface: |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ}sin(θ/2)|1⟩, after removing normalization and global phase.
  • Half-angles mean orthogonal states are antipodal; the Bloch vector is (⟨X⟩, ⟨Y⟩, ⟨Z⟩).
  • Pauli gates are π rotations about their axes; Z changes only phase and has no classical counterpart.
  • H exchanges the x and z axes, creating superposition and converting phase back to amplitude — the core of every algorithm in this course.
  • Clifford gates (H, S, CNOT) are classically simulable; T supplies universality and dominates fault-tolerant cost.

A1 — Z is a relative-phase operation, and relative phase is invisible to measurement in the basis it acts on but fully visible after a basis change. Measuring Z|ψ⟩ in the computational basis gives outcome probabilities |α|² and |β|², identical to |ψ⟩ — the sign on β vanishes under the modulus.

The minimal circuit that exposes it is H–Z–H, which equals X:

    |0⟩ ──[H]──[Z]──[H]── = |1⟩          measured: 1 with certainty
    |0⟩ ──[H]───────[H]── = |0⟩          measured: 0 with certainty

This is the mechanism behind every algorithm in the course. Computation happens in phases; Hadamards convert phase into amplitude so it can be read. Deutsch's algorithm is exactly this pattern with an oracle in place of Z, and phase kickback is the name for the trick.

A2 — It implies that entanglement and superposition are not sufficient for quantum advantage. Clifford circuits generate both — CNOT on |+⟩|0⟩ produces a maximally entangled Bell state — yet the stabilizer formalism tracks the whole evolution with polynomial classical resources. A machine running only Clifford gates, however many qubits, offers no speedup.

What supplies the advantage is non-stabilizer resource, usually called magic, contributed by gates like T that rotate outside the Clifford group. The practical consequence is that T-count, not qubit count, is the meaningful measure of a fault-tolerant algorithm's cost — and in surface-code architectures T gates require magic-state distillation, consuming many physical qubits and cycles per logical T.

The point to carry into Week 16: "how many qubits" is close to the wrong question. A thousand-qubit Clifford-only device is classically simulable. The figures that matter are logical qubit count, gate fidelity, and T-count — which is why current NISQ hardware, measured in physical qubits, sits much further from useful cryptanalysis than headline numbers suggest.

Week 8

Multi-Qubit Systems, CNOT, and Qiskit

6, 8 October · Objectives 3, 4

One qubit has a sphere. Two qubits have a 6-parameter space with no three-dimensional picture, and the extra structure is entanglement. This week adds the two-qubit gate that creates it, and puts the toolchain in your hands.

Composite state spaces

Two qubits span a four-dimensional space:

    |ψ⟩ = a|00⟩ + b|01⟩ + c|10⟩ + d|11⟩       |a|²+|b|²+|c|²+|d|² = 1

Convention: |q₁q₀⟩ with qubit 0 rightmost — Qiskit's little-endian ordering, and a reliable source of off-by-one confusion when reading bitstrings off hardware.

The separability test from Week 4, made computational: the state factors if and only if ad − bc = 0. That determinant is the two-qubit entanglement witness, and it is worth committing to memory:

    |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2  →  a=d=1/√2, b=c=0  →  ad−bc = 1/2 ≠ 0   entangled
    |+⟩|0⟩ = (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2 →  a=c=1/√2, b=d=0  →  ad−bc = 0        separable

CNOT and the entangling gates

CNOT flips the target if the control is |1⟩:

              [1 0 0 0]
    CNOT =    [0 1 0 0]        |00⟩→|00⟩    |10⟩→|11⟩
              [0 0 0 1]        |01⟩→|01⟩    |11⟩→|10⟩
              [0 0 1 0]

On basis states it is classical XOR. On superpositions it is the entangling engine:

    |+⟩|0⟩ = (|00⟩+|10⟩)/√2  --CNOT-->  (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 = |Φ⁺⟩

One Hadamard and one CNOT produce a Bell state. That two-gate circuit is the "hello world" of quantum computing and the basis of teleportation, superdense coding, and every entanglement benchmark you will run on hardware.

Phase kickback, which will matter enormously in Week 11: apply CNOT with the target in |−⟩:

    |ψ⟩|−⟩  --CNOT-->  (−1)^{ψ} |ψ⟩|−⟩

The target is unchanged. The control picks up a phase. Information flows backwards relative to the gate's apparent direction — the control/target labels describe the computational basis, not a causal channel.

Analogy boundary — CNOT as a conditional. It reads like if control: flip(target), and on basis states that is exactly right. Two failures. First, on superpositions there is no branch taken — both branches evolve, and their relative phase persists and can later interfere. Second, phase kickback means your if modifies its own condition variable, which no conditional in any language does. Treat CNOT as a linear operator on a 4-D space, and it behaves; treat it as control flow, and phase kickback will look like magic.

Universality. CNOT plus arbitrary single-qubit rotations is universal — any n-qubit unitary decomposes into them. This is the quantum counterpart of NAND completeness, with the caveat that decompositions can be exponentially long. Efficiency is the whole game.

Qiskit

Inspect the state before measurement — the diagnostic you will use constantly:

from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector, partial_trace, entropy

psi = Statevector(QuantumCircuit(2).compose(qc.remove_final_measurements(inplace=False)))
# Equivalent and cleaner:
bell = QuantumCircuit(2); bell.h(0); bell.cx(0, 1)
psi = Statevector(bell)
print(psi)                       # [0.707+0j, 0, 0, 0.707+0j]

rho0 = partial_trace(psi, [1])   # trace out qubit 1
print(entropy(rho0))             # 1.0 — maximally mixed, i.e. maximally entangled

That entropy value is the point. Qubit 0 alone carries one full bit of entropy — no information at all — while the pair is in a perfectly known pure state. All the information lives in the correlation.

Those ~4% wrong outcomes are the subject of Week 13. On an ideal device they are zero; on real hardware they measure your gate and readout fidelity.

Analogy boundary — transpilation as compilation. The parallel is close: lower an abstract program onto a target ISA, optimize, allocate registers. Where it diverges is connectivity. Two qubits can only interact if physically coupled, so the transpiler inserts SWAP networks to move states adjacent — and each SWAP is three CNOTs, each with error. A compiler's register allocation costs you spills; a transpiler's routing costs you fidelity, and on NISQ hardware routing overhead often dominates the algorithm itself.

Key takeaways

  • n qubits span 2ⁿ dimensions; Qiskit orders bitstrings little-endian, qubit 0 rightmost.
  • Two-qubit separability test: ad − bc = 0. Nonzero means entangled.
  • H followed by CNOT produces a Bell state — the canonical entangling circuit.
  • Phase kickback puts the phase on the control; CNOT is a linear operator, not control flow.
  • CNOT plus single-qubit rotations is universal. Transpilation costs fidelity through SWAP routing, not just time.

Check your answers

A1 — The hardware did not produce a different pure state; it produced a mixed state — a statistical ensemble. The ideal output is the pure |Φ⁺⟩; the device output is approximately ρ = (1−p)|Φ⁺⟩⟨Φ⁺| + p·(noise), with p ≈ 0.09 here. That is a different kind of object, not merely a different vector, and no state preparation error alone accounts for it.

The two independent sources:

Gate error — imperfect H and CNOT operations. The two-qubit gate dominates: CNOT error rates run ~10⁻³–10⁻² on current hardware, roughly an order of magnitude worse than single-qubit gates. Coherent errors (systematic over- or under-rotation) and incoherent errors (decoherence during the gate) both contribute.

Readout error — misclassification at measurement. The discriminator assigns |0⟩ or |1⟩ from a noisy analog signal, and confuses them at ~1–3%. Critically, this error occurs after the state is correct, so it is separable from gate error and correctable by different means.

Distinguishing them experimentally: run a circuit with no gates, preparing |00⟩ and measuring immediately. Any counts other than 00 are pure readout error. Subtract that baseline and the remainder is gate error. This is the basis of measurement-error mitigation, and it is the first diagnostic to run when hardware results look wrong.

A2 — Both statements are true and describe different systems. The pair is in a pure state |Φ⁺⟩ — complete knowledge, von Neumann entropy S(ρ_AB) = 0. The single qubit has ρ_A = tr_B(|Φ⁺⟩⟨Φ⁺|) = I/2, which is maximally mixed, S(ρ_A) = 1 bit.

There is no conservation violation because von Neumann entropy is not additive across subsystems for entangled states. Classically, S(AB) ≥ max(S(A), S(B)) — a whole is at least as uncertain as its parts. Quantum mechanically that inequality can be violated, and here it is maximally violated: 0 ≥ 1 is false. This is exactly the signature of entanglement, quantified as the entanglement entropy.

The resolution is that the information is neither in A nor in B. It is in the correlation, which the partial trace discards by construction. Tracing out B is the formal statement "I have no access to B," and under that restriction A genuinely is random — not as a description of your ignorance about a hidden value, but as a complete description of the accessible system.

The provenance framing: it is a record whose every field reads as noise, yet which is bit-exact against a counterpart record held elsewhere. The integrity lives in the joint object, and querying either half alone destroys the ability to see it.

A worked circuit: GHZ state

The three-qubit generalization of a Bell state, (|000⟩ + |111⟩)/√2:

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator

def ghz(n):
    qc = QuantumCircuit(n)
    qc.h(0)                      # seed the superposition on one qubit
    for q in range(n - 1):
        qc.cx(q, q + 1)          # propagate correlation down the chain
    return qc

qc = ghz(3)
print(Statevector(qc))
# [0.707+0j, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0.707+0j]  -> only |000⟩ and |111⟩ have amplitude

The linear chain of CNOTs is the readable construction, not the fastest. On hardware with limited connectivity a logarithmic-depth version wins:

def ghz_log_depth(n):
    qc = QuantumCircuit(n)
    qc.h(0)
    step = 1
    while step < n:              # double the entangled block each round
        for q in range(0, min(step, n - step)):
            qc.cx(q, q + step)
        step *= 2
    return qc

Depth O(log n) instead of O(n) — which matters because coherence time is fixed, so shallower circuits accumulate less decoherence. Depth, not gate count, is the quantity to minimize on NISQ hardware.

Verifying against theory

Never trust a circuit you have not checked. Three levels:

from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector, Operator, state_fidelity
import numpy as np

# 1. State-level: does it produce the state you intended?
target = Statevector([1/np.sqrt(2), 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1/np.sqrt(2)])
print(state_fidelity(Statevector(ghz(3)), target))     # 1.0

# 2. Operator-level: is the whole unitary what you think?
#    Stronger than state checks — it verifies behavior on ALL inputs.
print(Operator(qc_a).equiv(Operator(qc_b)))            # True if equal up to global phase

# 3. Structural: what will actually run?
qc_t = transpile(qc, backend, optimization_level=3)
print(qc_t.depth(), qc_t.count_ops())

Level 2 is the one people skip. A circuit can produce the right output on |000⟩ and be wrong everywhere else — which surfaces only when you use it as a subroutine.

Analogy boundary — statevector inspection as a debugger. Reading amplitudes mid-circuit feels like inspecting variables at a breakpoint, and for simulation the analogy is exact. It fails completely on hardware: there is no way to observe a real quantum state without measuring and destroying it. Statevector debugging is available only in the classical simulation of your circuit — which means it stops working precisely when your circuit gets big enough to be interesting. Design for verifiability at small n, then scale.

Measurement and shot statistics

Sampling error falls as 1/√N. To resolve a probability to ±ε you need N ≈ 1/ε² shots:

    ±0.10  →      100 shots
    ±0.01  →   10,000 shots
    ±0.001 → 1,000,000 shots

This is a hard economic constraint. Variational algorithms requiring many expectation values to high precision can spend hours of device time on a single optimization step, and it is the reason "shots" appears in every hardware cost model.

sim = AerSimulator()
qc_m = ghz(3); qc_m.measure_all()
for shots in (100, 10_000):
    c = sim.run(transpile(qc_m, sim), shots=shots).result().get_counts()
    p = c.get('000', 0) / shots
    print(f"{shots:>6}: P(000) = {p:.4f}  (expect 0.5000)")

Quiz 2 review

Covering mathematical foundations, gates, and circuits.

  • Bloch half-angles. |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ}sin(θ/2)|1⟩. Orthogonal ⇒ antipodal.
  • Gate identities. HZH = X, HXH = Z, S² = Z, T⁴ = Z, T⁸ = I. All Paulis square to I.
  • Separability. ad − bc = 0 ⟺ product state.
  • Little-endian. Qiskit prints qubit 0 rightmost.
  • Clifford vs. T. H, S, CNOT are classically simulable; T supplies universality.
  • Hermitian = observable, unitary = evolution. The Paulis are both.
  • Depth vs. count. Depth is the coherence-limited quantity.

Key takeaways

  • Verify with Statevector before spending shots; exact amplitudes expose logic errors that sampling noise hides.
  • Operator.equiv checks behavior on all inputs — strictly stronger than checking one output state.
  • Circuit depth governs decoherence exposure; log-depth constructions beat linear ones on hardware.
  • Shot count scales as 1/ε². Precision is purchased at quadratic cost.
  • Statevector debugging exists only in simulation, and disappears exactly when circuits become large enough to matter.

Check your answers

A1 — state_fidelity on one input checks a single column of the unitary. Two circuits agreeing on |000⟩ may disagree on every other basis state, and the difference surfaces the moment either is used as a controlled subroutine or applied to a superposed input — which is exactly how subroutines are used in Grover and Shor.

Two tests, in increasing strength:

# Operator equivalence: compares the full 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ unitary, all inputs at once.
Operator(qc_a).equiv(Operator(qc_b))     # up to global phase

# If that is too large, sample random inputs — cheaper, probabilistic.
for _ in range(20):

    assert state_fidelity(psi.evolve(qc_a), psi.evolve(qc_b)) > 1 - 1e-9

Beyond correctness, they are not interchangeable operationally even if the unitaries match exactly. The log-depth version has shorter depth, so it accumulates less decoherence; the linear version may transpile better on a linear-topology device by requiring no SWAPs. Scaling up:

    40,000 shots × 200 estimates × 50 steps = 400,000,000 shots

At a realistic 1–10 kHz effective sampling rate including reset and readout, that is 11 to 110 hours of device time — for one optimization run, assuming no queue and no re-runs. This is the central practical obstacle to variational quantum algorithms, and it is usually understated in papers that report only circuit depth.

The mitigations, all partial: reduce precision in early optimization steps and tighten as you converge; group commuting observables so one set of shots yields several expectation values; use shot-frugal optimizers (SPSA, Rosalin) that tolerate noisy gradients; and reduce the observable count through symmetry.

The lesson worth carrying: quantum advantage claims must account for shot overhead. An algorithm with an asymptotic speedup that needs 10⁸ samples per step may lose to a classical method that needs none — and comparisons that omit shot cost are not comparisons.

Week 10

Measurement, Collapse, and Reading Output

20, 22 October · Objectives 2, 4 Tuesday 20 October: Fall Break — no class

Measurement is where a quantum computation becomes a classical result, and it is the only lossy step in the pipeline. This week makes the projection formalism concrete and connects it to the counts dictionary you actually get back.

Projective measurement

Measuring observable Â = Σₘ aₘ|m⟩⟨m| on |ψ⟩:

    Outcome:      aₘ      with probability   P(m) = |⟨m|ψ⟩|²
    Post-state:   |m⟩     (the projector applied and renormalized)

More generally, with projector P̂ₘ onto the eigenspace of aₘ:

    P(m) = ⟨ψ|P̂ₘ|ψ⟩            |ψ'⟩ = P̂ₘ|ψ⟩ / √P(m)

Two properties define the operation:

Repeatability. Measure again immediately and you get the same result with certainty — the state is now an eigenstate. This is what makes measurement a projection rather than a random scramble.

Irreversibility. P̂ₘ is not unitary. P̂ₘ² = P̂ₘ (idempotent), and it has zero eigenvalues, so it has no inverse. The information in the discarded components is gone.

Analogy boundary — collapse as a state-machine commit. The parallel is strong: observation transitions the system to a definite state, and prior alternatives become unreachable — exactly your audit-trail write. Two boundaries. First, the timing of collapse is interpretation-dependent (Week 3); the formalism specifies outcome statistics, not a moment of transition. Second, your commit selects among branches that were separately real; quantum measurement selects among branches that were interfering, and could have cancelled. There was no fact about which branch was "actual" beforehand — Bell's theorem forecloses that reading.

Basis choice is the experiment

Hardware measures in the computational (Z) basis only. Measuring anything else means rotating the state so your target basis becomes the Z basis:

    Measure X:   apply H,        then measure Z
    Measure Y:   apply S† then H, then measure Z
# ⟨X⟩ on |+⟩ : rotate X-basis into Z-basis, then measure.
qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
qc.h(0)                 # prepare |+⟩
qc.h(0)                 # basis rotation: X-eigenbasis -> Z-eigenbasis
qc.measure(0, 0)
# Result: '0' with certainty. |+⟩ is a definite state in the X basis.

The same |+⟩ gives 50/50 in Z and certainty in X. Superposition is basis-relative — Week 4's claim, now as an executable demonstration.

Reading the counts dictionary

{'00': 512, '11': 488}

Three facts to keep straight:

Little-endian. '01' means qubit 1 = 0, qubit 0 = 1. Reversed from the natural reading, and the single most common bug in student code.

Counts are samples, not probabilities. With N shots, the standard error on each estimated probability is √(p(1−p)/N). At N = 1024 and p = 0.5, that is ±1.6% — so a 48/52 split is entirely consistent with a fair distribution.

Classical registers are indexed independently of qubits. measure(2, 0) puts qubit 2's result in classical bit 0. Multiple registers print space-separated, most-significant first.

from qiskit.result import marginal_counts
marginal_counts(counts, indices=[0])     # distribution over qubit 0 alone

Partial measurement

Measuring one qubit of an entangled pair collapses both:

qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 1)
qc.h(0); qc.cx(0, 1)      # |Φ⁺⟩
qc.measure(0, 0)          # measure ONLY qubit 0
# Qubit 1 is now determined: |0⟩ or |1⟩, matching qubit 0, though never measured.

Formally, the post-measurement state of the unmeasured qubit follows from applying P̂ₘ ⊗ I and renormalizing. This is the mechanism behind teleportation and behind the syndrome extraction of Week 16 — measuring ancillas to learn about data qubits without touching them.

Deferred measurement

A useful theorem: any measurement followed by a classically-controlled operation can be replaced by a coherent controlled operation, with all measurements moved to the end.

    ─[M]─●─         ≡        ──■──[M]─
         │                     │
    ─────X─                  ──X──────

This matters practically. Mid-circuit measurement with feed-forward requires fast classical control that many devices lack or implement slowly; the deferred form runs on any device. Theoretically, it means measurement is never necessary mid-computation — a fact used constantly in proofs.

Interpreting noisy output

Expected for a Bell state; observed on hardware:

    ideal:     {'00': 512, '11': 512}
    hardware:  {'00': 470, '11': 468, '01': 45, '10': 41}

  1. Baseline readout error. Run an empty circuit preparing |00⟩. Any non-00 counts are pure readout error, correctable by inverting the confusion matrix.

Key takeaways

  • Measurement applies a projector: outcome with probability ⟨ψ|P̂ₘ|ψ⟩, state renormalized to the eigenspace. Repeatable, and not unitary.
  • Hardware measures in Z only; other bases require an explicit rotation first (H for X, S†H for Y).
  • Counts are samples with standard error √(p(1−p)/N); Qiskit bitstrings are little-endian.
  • Measuring one qubit of an entangled pair determines the other without touching it.
  • Deferred measurement lets any mid-circuit measurement move to the end — essential on devices without fast feed-forward.

Week 11

Feynman's Vision and Deutsch's Algorithm

27, 29 October · Objectives 6, 8

Feynman's 1981 observation was that simulating quantum systems on classical hardware costs exponentially, so the machine should be quantum. Deutsch turned that into the first algorithm with a provable quantum advantage — small, artificial, and containing the complete pattern every later algorithm follows.

Feynman's argument

Representing n qubits classically requires 2ⁿ complex amplitudes. Fifty spins need 2⁵⁰ ≈ 10¹⁵ numbers; a hundred exceeds the atom count of any storage medium you could build. Chemistry and materials science therefore hit an exponential wall on classical hardware.

Feynman's proposal, at MIT's 1981 Physics of Computation conference: use a controllable quantum system as the simulator. "Nature isn't classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical."

The framing that holds up: this is a native-execution argument, not an acceleration argument. You are not speeding up a classical algorithm; you are running the problem on hardware whose state space has the same structure as the problem's. Quantum chemistry remains the most credible near-term application for exactly this reason — the mapping is natural rather than contrived.

The oracle model

Deutsch's problem: given a black-box function f: {0,1} → {0,1}, decide whether it is constant (f(0) = f(1)) or balanced (f(0) ≠ f(1)).

Four possible functions; two constant, two balanced. Classically you must evaluate f twice — one evaluation leaves both cases possible. One query is provably insufficient.

The quantum oracle must be unitary, hence reversible. The standard construction:

    U_f |x⟩|y⟩ = |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩

XOR onto an ancilla, preserving x. This is reversible (apply twice to get the identity) and is the generic recipe for embedding any classical function in a quantum circuit.

Analogy boundary — the oracle as a function call. U_f is a callable with a fixed signature, and query counting is a clean complexity measure. Two boundaries. First, U_f must be reversible, so it cannot discard inputs — every classical function needs the ancilla embedding, and that is a real cost. Second, and more important: you may call it on a superposition of arguments, which has no software analogue. That is not "calling it many times fast." It is one invocation whose input is a single state that happens not to be a basis state, and the distinction matters for reasoning about cost.

Phase kickback

The mechanism that makes the algorithm work. Set the ancilla to |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2:

    U_f |x⟩|−⟩ = |x⟩ · (|0 ⊕ f(x)⟩ − |1 ⊕ f(x)⟩)/√2

If f(x) = 0 the ancilla is unchanged. If f(x) = 1 the two terms swap, which is an overall sign flip. Combining:

    U_f |x⟩|−⟩ = (−1)^{f(x)} |x⟩|−⟩

The ancilla is untouched. The function's output has become a phase on the input register. This is the same effect seen with CNOT in Week 8, and it is the workhorse of quantum algorithms: it converts a value you cannot read into a phase you can interfere.

Deutsch's algorithm

        ┌───┐      ┌─────┐      ┌───┐ ┌─┐
  |0⟩ ──┤ H ├──────┤     ├──────┤ H ├─┤M├
        └───┘      │ U_f │      └───┘ └─┘
        ┌───┐┌───┐ │     │
  |1⟩ ──┤ X ├┤ H ├─┤     ├───────────────
        └───┘└───┘ └─────┘

Step by step. After the Hadamards, the input register is |+⟩ and the ancilla is |−⟩:

    (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 ⊗ |−⟩

Apply U_f, using phase kickback:

    → ((−1)^{f(0)}|0⟩ + (−1)^{f(1)}|1⟩)/√2 ⊗ |−⟩

Factor out the global phase (−1)^{f(0)}, which is unobservable:

    → (|0⟩ + (−1)^{f(0)⊕f(1)}|1⟩)/√2

The relative phase now encodes f(0) ⊕ f(1) — precisely the quantity asked for. Apply H:

    f(0) ⊕ f(1) = 0  (constant)  →  |+⟩ → |0⟩
    f(0) ⊕ f(1) = 1  (balanced)  →  |−⟩ → |1⟩

Measure. One query, deterministic answer.

Note what happened: at no point did you learn f(0) or f(1) individually. The algorithm extracts a global property of the function while leaving its values inaccessible. That trade is the essence of quantum algorithms — you get one bit about the whole function, not the function.

Analogy boundary — "evaluating both inputs at once." The standard gloss is that the superposition evaluates f on 0 and 1 simultaneously. This is misleading in a way that will cost you later. Both amplitudes evolve, yes — but you can never read both results. The algorithm works because the final Hadamard interferes them, and the interference is constructive exactly when the answer is one bit. Drop the H and you get a random bit and learn nothing. Parallelism is not the resource; engineered interference is.

Deutsch–Jozsa

The n-bit generalization. Given f: {0,1}ⁿ → {0,1}, promised to be either constant or balanced (exactly half the inputs map to 1), decide which.

Classically, worst case requires 2ⁿ⁻¹ + 1 queries for certainty. Quantum: one query, by the same H → U_f → H structure on n qubits. An exponential separation in exact query complexity.

The caveat that matters for Week 16's honesty: with randomization, a classical algorithm decides this with high probability in O(1) queries — sample a few inputs and see if they differ. The exponential gap holds only for deterministic exact classical computation. Deutsch– Jozsa proves quantum computers are provably different, not that they are practically useful for this task. It is a proof of concept, and it should be described as one.

Key takeaways

  • Feynman's argument is about native execution: quantum systems cost exponential classical memory, so simulate them on quantum hardware.
  • Reversible oracles use the XOR embedding U_f|x⟩|y⟩ = |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩, requiring an ancilla.
  • Phase kickback with the ancilla in |−⟩ converts function output into an input-register phase: U_f|x⟩|−⟩ = (−1)^{f(x)}|x⟩|−⟩.
  • Deutsch decides constant vs. balanced in one query; the final Hadamard converts phase into a measurable bit.
  • Deutsch–Jozsa gives exponential separation against deterministic classical algorithms only. Randomized classical algorithms close the gap.

Check your answers

A1 — Without the final H you measure the state (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2 in the computational basis and get 0 or 1 with equal probability, regardless of f. Both the constant and balanced cases yield a uniform random bit, and the outcome carries no information about the function.

The reason: the answer is encoded in the relative phase between |0⟩ and |1⟩, and computational-basis measurement is blind to relative phase — |⟨0|ψ⟩|² = |⟨1|ψ⟩|² = 1/2 for both |+⟩ and |−⟩. The information is present in the state and inaccessible to that measurement.

This establishes that quantum advantage comes from interference, not superposition. The superposition existed and the oracle was queried on it, so if parallel evaluation were the resource, the answer would be available. It is not. The Hadamard rotates the {|+⟩, |−⟩} basis — in which the two cases are orthogonal and perfectly distinguishable — onto the computational basis the hardware can read. Every algorithm in this course ends with such a rotation: Grover's diffusion operator and Shor's inverse QFT are both this step generalized.

A2 — Two independent reasons.

The problem is artificial. The promise that f is either constant or exactly balanced — never anything else — is not a property real functions have. Deutsch–Jozsa solves a problem constructed to be solvable, which makes it a proof of separation rather than an application.

Randomization closes the gap. Sample two random inputs. If they differ, f is balanced — certain. If they agree, guess constant. For a balanced f, each pair agrees with probability ≈ 1/2, so k samples give error ≤ 2^{−k}. Ten queries yield error under 0.1%, independent of n. The exponential separation exists only against algorithms required to be exactly correct with certainty, which is an unusual and rarely-needed requirement.

For a separation to survive randomization, the problem needs structure that random sampling cannot exploit — typically a global, periodic, or algebraic property invisible in any small set of evaluations. Simon's problem is the clean example: it has a hidden XOR-mask period, requires exponentially many queries even for randomized classical algorithms, and directly inspired Shor's algorithm. Shor's period-finding is the same idea applied to modular exponentiation, and it is why factoring — not Deutsch–Jozsa — is the result that changed cryptography.

Week 12

Deutsch in Qiskit, and Real Hardware

3, 5 November · Objectives 5, 6

Building the four oracles, running the algorithm, and then running it somewhere the physics fights back. The gap between simulator and device is the actual subject of this week.

The four oracles

Each is a reversible circuit implementing U_f|x⟩|y⟩ = |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩:

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

def oracle(kind):
    """Two qubits: 0 = input x, 1 = ancilla y."""
    qc = QuantumCircuit(2, name=f"U_f[{kind}]")
    if kind == "constant_0":
        pass                     # f(x) = 0: identity, nothing to XOR
    elif kind == "constant_1":
        qc.x(1)                  # f(x) = 1: flip ancilla unconditionally
    elif kind == "balanced_id":
        qc.cx(0, 1)              # f(x) = x: XOR input onto ancilla
    elif kind == "balanced_not":
        qc.cx(0, 1); qc.x(1)     # f(x) = NOT x
    return qc

Note that the two constant oracles never touch qubit 0. Whatever the algorithm learns, it learns from a gate that does not act on the register being measured — which is phase kickback stated as an observation about the circuit diagram.

The algorithm

def deutsch(kind):
    qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 1)

    qc.x(1); qc.h(1)             # ancilla -> |−⟩, arming phase kickback
    qc.h(0)                      # input   -> |+⟩, both branches live

    qc.compose(oracle(kind), inplace=True)

    qc.h(0)                      # interfere: relative phase -> amplitude
    qc.measure(0, 0)
    return qc

from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit import transpile

sim = AerSimulator()
for kind in ["constant_0", "constant_1", "balanced_id", "balanced_not"]:
    qc = deutsch(kind)
    counts = sim.run(transpile(qc, sim), shots=1024).result().get_counts()
    verdict = "constant" if max(counts, key=counts.get) == "0" else "balanced"
    print(f"{kind:<14} {counts}  ->  {verdict}")
constant_0     {'0': 1024}   ->  constant
constant_1     {'0': 1024}   ->  constant
balanced_id    {'1': 1024}   ->  balanced

Deterministic — every shot agrees. Interference has made the answer certain, not merely likely. Any deviation on hardware is therefore pure error, which makes this circuit an unusually clean diagnostic.

Verifying the phase

The ancilla returns unchanged in |−⟩ while the input register acquired a relative phase. Nothing was written to the ancilla; the function's value lives entirely in a sign on the register that was never the gate's target.

Five percent wrong on a circuit this shallow is the number to sit with. The theoretical answer is deterministic; the device disagrees one time in twenty.

Where the error comes from

Budget it explicitly:

  SOURCE                  TYPICAL RATE      NOTES
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Single-qubit gate       1e-4 – 1e-3       usually negligible here
  Two-qubit gate (CX)     3e-3 – 1e-2       dominates gate error
  Readout                 1e-2 – 3e-2       often the largest term
  T1 (amplitude damping)  100–300 μs        |1⟩ decays toward |0⟩
  T2 (dephasing)          80–200 μs         relative phase randomizes

For Deutsch, with one CX and one measurement, readout error dominates. T2 matters more than T1 in principle, because the algorithm stores its answer in a phase — though at ~1 μs circuit duration against ~100 μs T2, decoherence contributes little here. On deeper circuits that ordering reverses.

Analogy boundary — decoherence as bit rot. Data degrading in storage is the right intuition for T1: |1⟩ relaxes toward |0⟩, an asymmetric, energy-losing process much like a biased corruption. T2 has no classical counterpart. Dephasing destroys relative phase while leaving every measurement probability in the computational basis unchanged. Your monitoring would report the data intact — populations correct, checksums matching in that basis — while the quantity the algorithm depends on has silently vanished. Detecting it requires measuring in a rotated basis, which is exactly what randomized benchmarking does.

Measurement error mitigation

Readout error is the cheapest to correct, because it is characterized independently of the circuit:

# Calibrate: prepare each basis state, measure, build the confusion matrix.
cal = []
for bits in ["0", "1"]:
    c = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
    if bits == "1": c.x(0)
    c.measure(0, 0)
    cal.append(transpile(c, backend, optimization_level=3))

# The result is P(measured | prepared). Inverting it and applying to your
# raw counts recovers an estimate of the true distribution.

Qiskit Runtime applies this automatically via resilience_level=1. Note the limit: mitigation is post-processing on the distribution, so it improves estimates while amplifying statistical noise, and it does nothing for gate error. It is not error correction — that is Week 16, and it is a different kind of thing entirely.

Key takeaways

  • The constant oracles never act on the measured register; the answer arrives entirely by phase kickback.
  • Deutsch's output is deterministic in theory, which makes any hardware deviation a direct error measurement.
  • optimization_level=3 is mandatory on hardware — routing and gate cancellation change depth substantially.
  • On shallow circuits readout error usually dominates; two-qubit gate error dominates as depth grows.
  • T2 dephasing destroys phase while leaving computational-basis statistics intact — invisible to the obvious diagnostic.

A2 — Decoherence during the circuit is governed by the ratio of duration to coherence time:

    phase coherence:  exp(−t/T2) = exp(−2/120)  ≈ 0.983   → ~1.7% loss
    amplitude decay:  exp(−t/T1) = exp(−2/200)  ≈ 0.990   → ~1.0% loss

Why T2 ≤ 2·T1 always. T1 is energy relaxation — the |1⟩ population decaying to |0⟩. T2 is the loss of phase coherence, and it has two independent causes: energy relaxation (any T1 event destroys phase, since the state is gone) plus pure dephasing T_φ from noise that randomizes phase without exchanging energy. They combine as:

    1/T2 = 1/(2·T1) + 1/T_φ

Since 1/T_φ ≥ 0, we get T2 ≤ 2·T1. The pure-dephasing channel is usually dominant in superconducting qubits — flux noise, charge noise, and thermal photons in the readout resonator all randomize phase without causing a transition. That is why T2 = 120 μs sits well below the 2·T1 = 400 μs ceiling, and why dynamical decoupling, which targets T_φ specifically, is worth the trouble.

Week 13

Noise, Decoherence, and Grover's Algorithm

10, 12 November · Objectives 5, 7

Two halves that belong together: the physics limiting how long a computation can run, and the first algorithm whose speedup applies to problems anyone actually has.

Modelling noise

Noise is described by quantum channels — completely positive trace-preserving maps built from Kraus operators:

    ε(ρ) = Σₖ Eₖ ρ Eₖ†          with Σₖ Eₖ†Eₖ = I

The three that matter:

Amplitude damping (T1) — energy loss to the environment. Asymmetric: |1⟩ decays to |0⟩, never the reverse (at zero temperature).

    E₀ = [1    0    ]      E₁ = [0  √γ]        γ = 1 − e^{−t/T1}
         [0  √(1−γ)]           [0   0 ]

Phase damping (T2) — coherence loss with no energy exchange. Populations are untouched; off-diagonal elements shrink:

    ρ = [ρ₀₀  ρ₀₁]   →   [ρ₀₀        ρ₀₁·e^{−t/T2}]
        [ρ₁₀  ρ₁₁]       [ρ₁₀·e^{−t/T2}   ρ₁₁     ]

Depolarizing — the state is replaced by the maximally mixed state with probability p:

    ε(ρ) = (1−p)ρ + p·I/2

On the Bloch sphere, all three shrink the vector inward. Amplitude damping pulls toward the north pole; phase damping collapses the sphere toward the z-axis; depolarizing shrinks uniformly toward the center. Decoherence is the state vector leaving the surface — and points inside the ball are mixed states, which is where the picture from Week 7 pays off.

Analogy boundary — noise as a corruption rate. A per-operation error probability is the right first model, and error budgets built that way are useful. Two boundaries. First, quantum errors are continuous — a gate can over-rotate by 0.03 radians, which is not a discrete bit flip and has no classical counterpart. (That error correction works anyway is the surprising result of Week 16: syndrome measurement digitizes continuous errors.) Second, coherent errors accumulate linearly in amplitude, hence quadratically in probability. A thousand gates each over-rotating by the same small angle compound into a large systematic error, far worse than a thousand independent random ones — which is why randomized compiling, deliberately converting coherent error into stochastic error, is a real technique.

Building a noise model

from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit_aer.noise import NoiseModel, depolarizing_error, thermal_relaxation_error

nm.add_all_qubit_quantum_error(depolarizing_error(0.001, 1), ['h', 'x', 'rz'])
nm.add_all_qubit_quantum_error(depolarizing_error(0.010, 2), ['cx'])
nm.add_all_qubit_quantum_error(
    thermal_relaxation_error(t1=200e-6, t2=120e-6, time=500e-9), ['cx'])

sim = AerSimulator(noise_model=nm)

# Or, more usefully, clone a real device's calibrated error rates:
sim = AerSimulator.from_backend(backend)

The second form is the one to use before spending queue time — it reproduces the device's actual per-qubit rates and reveals whether a circuit is feasible at all.

Grover's algorithm

Problem. Search N unstructured items for one satisfying f(x) = 1. Classically: N/2 expected queries. Grover: O(√N).

For a database of 10⁶ items, 10⁶ classical queries become ~10³. Quadratic, not exponential — and unlike Deutsch–Jozsa, the speedup survives randomization. It is also provably optimal: no quantum algorithm does unstructured search faster (Bennett–Bernstein–Brassard–Vazirani, 1997).

The geometry. Work in the two-dimensional plane spanned by the target |w⟩ and the uniform superposition of everything else |s'⟩. Start in the uniform state |s⟩, at angle θ from |s'⟩ where sin θ = 1/√N.

Each Grover iteration is two reflections:

    1. ORACLE:     reflect about |s'⟩     (flip the sign of |w⟩)
    2. DIFFUSION:  reflect about |s⟩      (invert about the mean)

Two reflections compose into a rotation — by 2θ toward |w⟩ each time:

        |w⟩
         │        ●  after k iterations
         │      ╱
         │    ╱  (2k+1)θ
         │  ╱
         │╱
    ─────┴──────────── |s'⟩

After k iterations the amplitude on |w⟩ is sin((2k+1)θ). Maximize it:

    (2k+1)θ ≈ π/2      ⟹      k ≈ (π/4)√N

Overshooting is real. Run too many iterations and the state rotates past |w⟩ and success probability falls. This is genuinely counterintuitive — more work makes the answer worse — and it is a favorite exam question.

Analogy boundary — Grover as database search. The name misleads. Grover does not search a stored database; it searches the domain of a function you can evaluate. To search actual stored data you would first load N items into quantum superposition, which costs O(N) — destroying the speedup. Grover applies where the "database" is implicit: satisfying assignments of a formula, keys of a cipher, positions in a game tree. Nothing you would put in a table.

The circuit

import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

def grover(n, marked):
    N = 2**n
    k = int(np.floor(np.pi/4 * np.sqrt(N)))
    qc = QuantumCircuit(n, n)
    qc.h(range(n))                       # uniform superposition over all N

    for _ in range(k):
        # --- oracle: phase-flip the marked state ---
        for q in range(n):
            if not (marked >> q) & 1: qc.x(q)
        qc.h(n-1); qc.mcx(list(range(n-1)), n-1); qc.h(n-1)   # multi-controlled Z
        for q in range(n):
            if not (marked >> q) & 1: qc.x(q)

        # --- diffusion: reflect about the uniform state ---
        qc.h(range(n)); qc.x(range(n))
        qc.h(n-1); qc.mcx(list(range(n-1)), n-1); qc.h(n-1)
        qc.x(range(n)); qc.h(range(n))

    qc.measure(range(n), range(n))
    return qc, k

qc, k = grover(4, marked=11)     # 16 items, target |1011⟩
print(f"{k} iterations for N=16")     # 3
3 iterations for N=16
{'1011': 3961, ...}   ≈ 96.7% success

Key takeaways

  • Noise is modelled by Kraus channels: amplitude damping (T1), phase damping (T2), and depolarizing. All shrink the Bloch vector inward.
  • Coherent errors compound quadratically in probability; randomized compiling converts them to stochastic error deliberately.
  • Grover searches in O(√N) — quadratic, provably optimal, and robust to randomization.
  • Each iteration is two reflections composing to a rotation of 2θ; run ≈ (π/4)√N of them.
  • Overshooting reduces success probability. More iterations is not monotonically better.

Grover in Practice, and Shor's Algorithm

Grover on hardware, then the algorithm that made quantum computing a policy question. Shor's is the one result in this course with a deadline attached to it.

Grover on hardware

The multi-controlled Z gates dominate cost, and their expense is easy to underestimate:

# CX count: 214

An n-controlled X decomposes into O(n) Toffolis, each needing 6 CNOTs, and every CNOT carries ~10⁻² error. With 214 CX gates:

    (1 − 0.01)^214 ≈ 0.115

About 12% of runs survive without a two-qubit error. Measured success on a real device runs 20–40% for n = 4 — better than the naive estimate, since not every gate error flips the outcome, but far from the simulator's 96.7%.

Ancilla-assisted decomposition trades qubits for depth:

qc.mcx(controls, target, mode='v-chain', ancilla_qubits=anc)
# O(n) CX and O(n) depth, using n−2 ancillas — versus O(n²) CX without them.

On NISQ hardware this trade is usually correct: idle ancillas decohere, but a shallower circuit decoheres less overall.

Analogy boundary — gate count as instruction count. Counting gates like instructions gives a reasonable first cost model, and depth-versus-count maps onto latency-versus-throughput. It fails on error compounding: instructions do not have a probability of silently corrupting the machine. Success falls off exponentially in gate count, so a circuit 2× longer is not 2× slower — it may be qualitatively infeasible. There is no NISQ equivalent of "let it run longer."

Shor's algorithm

Problem. Factor a composite N. Best classical algorithm (general number field sieve) runs in sub-exponential time; Shor runs in polynomial time — O((log N)³). This is an exponential speedup on a problem the world's public-key infrastructure depends on.

The reduction to period-finding. Factoring is not attacked directly. The structure:

  1. Pick random a with 1 < a < N.
  2. If gcd(a, N) > 1, that gcd is a factor. Done (rare).
  3. Find the PERIOD r of f(x) = aˣ mod N  —  the smallest r with aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N).
  4. If r is odd, or a^{r/2} ≡ −1 (mod N), restart with a different a.
  5. Otherwise gcd(a^{r/2} ± 1, N) are nontrivial factors.

Steps 1, 2, 4, and 5 are classical and cheap. Only step 3 is quantum. The algebra of step 5: if r is the period, then aʳ − 1 ≡ 0 (mod N), so N divides (a^{r/2} − 1)(a^{r/2} + 1). Provided neither factor is a multiple of N, each shares a nontrivial divisor with N.

Worked example, N = 15, a = 7:

    7¹ = 7,  7² = 4,  7³ = 13,  7⁴ = 1  (mod 15)      →  r = 4
    r even ✓,  7² = 4 ≢ −1 (mod 15) ✓
    gcd(7² − 1, 15) = gcd(48, 15) = 3
    gcd(7² + 1, 15) = gcd(50, 15) = 5           15 = 3 × 5  ✓

Why period-finding is quantum-hard

Classically, finding the period of aˣ mod N requires evaluating f at exponentially many points — the period can be as large as N, and there is no structure a classical algorithm can exploit to locate it faster.

Quantumly, prepare a superposition over all x, compute f(x) into a second register, and the first register is left in a periodic superposition. The quantum Fourier transform then converts period into frequency, which measurement can read.

    1. |0⟩|0⟩  --H⊗ⁿ-->  (1/√Q) Σₓ |x⟩|0⟩
    2. modular exponentiation:  (1/√Q) Σₓ |x⟩|aˣ mod N⟩
    3. measuring the second register collapses the first to
       a comb of states spaced r apart:  |x₀⟩ + |x₀+r⟩ + |x₀+2r⟩ + ...
    4. QFT⁻¹ maps the comb to peaks at multiples of Q/r
    5. continued fractions on the measured value recovers r

Step 3 is worth pausing on: measuring the second register — which you never look at again — is what imposes periodicity on the first. This is a constructive use of collapse, and it is where partial measurement from Week 10 earns its keep.

Analogy boundary — the QFT as an FFT. The QFT applies the same unitary as the DFT and does it in O((log Q)²) gates versus the FFT's O(Q log Q) — apparently an exponential speedup at signal processing. It is not, and the reason matters: the QFT's output amplitudes are not readable. You get one sample from the transformed distribution, not the spectrum. The QFT is useful only when the answer is a single dominant frequency you can identify from few samples. Shor's works because period-finding has exactly that structure. You cannot use the QFT to accelerate signal processing.

Cryptographic consequences

  PRIMITIVE            BASIS                QUANTUM STATUS
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  RSA                  factoring            BROKEN (Shor, polynomial)
  Diffie–Hellman       discrete log         BROKEN (Shor variant)
  ECDSA / ECDH         elliptic-curve DL    BROKEN (Shor variant)
  AES-128              brute force          weakened to ~2⁶⁴ (Grover)
  AES-256              brute force          ~2¹²⁸ — safe
  SHA-256              preimage             ~2¹²⁸ — safe

The asymmetry is the point: asymmetric cryptography falls exponentially, symmetric cryptography only quadratically. Doubling symmetric key lengths restores their margin. There is no equivalent patch for RSA or ECC — they require replacement, which is what NIST's post-quantum standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, finalized 2024) provide.

Harvest now, decrypt later. An adversary recording encrypted traffic today can decrypt it whenever a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. For data with a long confidentiality lifetime — health records, state secrets, provenance chains meant to hold for decades — the migration deadline is not the arrival of the machine. It is now minus the required secrecy duration. Anything you need confidential in 2050 needs post-quantum protection today.

Key takeaways

  • Grover's multi-controlled gates transpile to hundreds of CX; success falls exponentially in gate count. Ancilla-assisted decompositions trade qubits for depth, usually correctly.
  • Shor reduces factoring to period-finding; only that step is quantum, and it runs in O((log N)³).
  • Measuring the second register collapses the first into a periodic comb — a constructive use of measurement.
  • The QFT is not a faster FFT: its output is sampled, not read, so it helps only when a single frequency dominates.
  • RSA/DH/ECC break exponentially; AES and SHA lose only a quadratic factor. Harvest-now-decrypt- later moves the migration deadline into the present.

Implementing Shor: Quantum Period Finding

24, 26 November · Objective 7 Thursday 26 November: Thanksgiving Break — no class

Building the pieces: the quantum Fourier transform, modular exponentiation, and the continued- fraction post-processing that turns a measured integer into a period. Then an honest accounting of why nobody has factored anything interesting.

The quantum Fourier transform

The QFT maps computational basis states to Fourier basis states:

    QFT |x⟩ = (1/√Q) Σ_{y=0}^{Q−1} e^{2πixy/Q} |y⟩          Q = 2ⁿ

The circuit is Hadamards interleaved with controlled phase rotations:

import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

def qft(n):
    qc = QuantumCircuit(n, name="QFT")
    for j in range(n):
        qc.h(j)
        for k in range(j + 1, n):
            # Controlled rotation by π/2^(k−j): encodes the pairwise phase
            # relationship that makes the transform separable.
            qc.cp(np.pi / 2**(k - j), k, j)
    for j in range(n // 2):
        qc.swap(j, n - 1 - j)        # QFT reverses bit order
    return qc

O(n²) gates for a transform over 2ⁿ amplitudes — against the classical FFT's O(Q log Q). As Week 14 established, this is not usable for signal processing, because the output is sampled rather than read. It is usable when one frequency dominates.

Approximate QFT. Rotations by π/2^k with large k are below hardware precision and can be dropped, reducing to O(n log n) gates with negligible error. This is standard in resource estimates.

Modular exponentiation

The expensive part — and the reason Shor is hard to run. You need a reversible circuit for:

    |x⟩|1⟩  →  |x⟩|aˣ mod N⟩

built from repeated controlled modular multiplication, each from controlled modular addition. Resource requirements for an n-bit N:

    Qubits:        ~2n + 3           (2n counting register + n work + ancillas)
    Toffoli gates: ~O(n³)
    For RSA-2048:  ~4100 logical qubits, ~10⁹ Toffoli gates

Modular exponentiation dominates the circuit — the QFT is a rounding error beside it. This is why "how many qubits to break RSA" has an answer in the thousands of logical qubits and the millions of physical ones.

Continued fractions

Measurement returns y ≈ (Q/r)·k for some integer k, so y/Q ≈ k/r. Recovering r from that approximation is a classical number-theory step:

from fractions import Fraction

def period_from_measurement(y, Q, N, a):
    frac = Fraction(y, Q).limit_denominator(N)     # continued-fraction convergent
    r = frac.denominator
    if r % 2 == 0 and pow(a, r, N) == 1:
        return r
    return None

limit_denominator implements the continued-fraction expansion, returning the best rational approximation with denominator below N. It succeeds with constant probability per run, so a few repetitions suffice.

Full implementation, N = 15

        if a in [2, 13]:   U.swap(2,3); U.swap(1,2); U.swap(0,1)
        if a in [7, 8]:    U.swap(0,1); U.swap(1,2); U.swap(2,3)
        if a in [4, 11]:   U.swap(1,3); U.swap(0,2)

    return qc

Analogy boundary — Shor as a factoring service. The amod15 shortcuts above are hardcoded permutations for one modulus, not a general implementation, and this is the standard practice in demonstrations. Published "factorizations" of 15, 21, and 35 have almost all used circuits compiled with knowledge of the answer — Smolin, Smith, and Vargo showed in 2013 that such demonstrations can be reduced to near-trivial circuits. No general-purpose Shor implementation has factored a number that classical methods could not factor instantly. When you read a factoring headline, the question to ask is whether the modular exponentiation was general or compiled against the known factors.

The factor of 2 shared by k = 2 and r = 4 cancels, and the information about r is destroyed by the reduction.

This is the general failure mode: measuring y = k·Q/r yields k/r in lowest terms, so whenever gcd(k, r) > 1 you recover a proper divisor of r rather than r itself. Here k = 2, gcd(2, 4) = 2, and you get r/2 = 2.

The remedy is built into the algorithm: verify and retry. Check pow(a, r, N) == 1; if it fails, rerun. Since the number of k values coprime to r is φ(r), the success probability per run is φ(r)/r, which is Ω(1/log log r) — good enough that a handful of runs suffices. You can also test small multiples of the recovered candidate, which often finds r without another quantum run. Note that y = 0 is likewise a legitimate peak carrying no information, for the same reason.

A2 — The counting register must resolve the period, and the requirement comes from the resolution of the Fourier peak.

The measured y approximates k·Q/r. To recover r by continued fractions you need y/Q close enough to k/r that k/r is the unique rational with denominator below N in that neighborhood. Two distinct fractions with denominators under N can differ by as little as:

    |k₁/r₁ − k₂/r₂| = |k₁r₂ − k₂r₁| / (r₁r₂)  ≥  1/N²

So you need y/Q accurate to better than 1/(2N²). With Q = 2^{n_count} and N ≈ 2ⁿ:

    1/Q < 1/(2N²) = 1/(2·2^{2n})     ⟹     n_count ≳ 2n + 1

Hence ~2n counting qubits. With only n, the resolution is ~1/N and multiple candidate fractions fall within the measurement's uncertainty — the continued-fraction step cannot distinguish them, and r is unrecoverable.

The intuition worth keeping: the counting register sets the frequency resolution of the QFT, exactly as record length sets resolution in a classical DFT. Periods up to N require resolving frequencies as fine as 1/N, and distinguishing nearby such frequencies requires 1/N² — so the register must be twice as wide as the number you are factoring. For RSA-2048 that is ~4096 counting qubits, which is where the ~4100 logical qubit figure originates.

Week 16

Error Correction, Limits, and Honest Assessment

1, 3 December · Objective 8 · Final project presentations

Everything so far assumed the computation survives. It does not. This week covers how error correction rescues it, what the overhead costs, and an assessment of where the field actually stands — which is the most professionally useful thing in the course.

Why quantum error correction is hard

Three obstacles that classical coding theory never faces:

  1. No-cloning. Repetition codes are unavailable. You cannot store three copies.
  2. Continuous errors. A gate can over-rotate by any angle — not a discrete bit flip.
  3. Measurement destroys. Reading a qubit to check it collapses the state you are protecting.

All three are circumvented by one idea: measure the error, not the state.

Stabilizer measurement

Encode one logical qubit across several physical ones, then measure operators that reveal whether an error occurred while revealing nothing about the logical state.

The three-qubit bit-flip code:

    |0⟩_L = |000⟩          |1⟩_L = |111⟩
    Stabilizers:  Z₀Z₁  and  Z₁Z₂

Measuring Z₀Z₁ asks "do qubits 0 and 1 agree?" — a parity check. It returns +1 or −1 and says nothing about whether the state is |000⟩, |111⟩, or a superposition. The syndrome localizes the error:

    Z₀Z₁   Z₁Z₂    diagnosis
    ─────────────────────────────
     +1     +1     no error
     −1     +1     qubit 0 flipped
     −1     −1     qubit 1 flipped
     +1     −1     qubit 2 flipped
qc = QuantumCircuit(5, 2)          # 3 data + 2 syndrome ancillas
qc.cx(0, 3); qc.cx(1, 3)           # ancilla 3 accumulates parity of Z₀Z₁
qc.cx(1, 4); qc.cx(2, 4)           # ancilla 4 accumulates parity of Z₁Z₂
qc.measure([3, 4], [0, 1])         # measure ancillas ONLY — data untouched

Continuous errors are digitized by this measurement. An error that rotates a qubit by small angle θ leaves it in a superposition of "no error" and "bit flip." Measuring the syndrome projects onto one or the other — with probability sin²θ you find a full flip and correct it; otherwise you find nothing and the state is restored exactly. The measurement discretizes the error, which is what makes a finite correction set sufficient. This is the single most elegant result in the subject.

Analogy boundary — syndromes as checksums. Parity checks over data blocks, with the syndrome identifying the corrupted position, is genuinely the same mathematics — the surface code is a CSS code built from two classical codes. Two boundaries. First, your checksum reads the data; a stabilizer measurement must extract parity without learning any data value, since that would collapse the logical state. Second, quantum codes correct two independent error types — bit flips (X) and phase flips (Z) — and correcting one can introduce the other. The three-qubit code above handles only bit flips; Shor's nine-qubit code concatenates two such codes to handle both.

The surface code

The leading architecture. Qubits on a 2-D lattice with only nearest-neighbour interactions — which matters because that is what hardware can build.

    ─●───□───●───□───●─        ● data qubit
     │   │   │   │   │         □ syndrome qubit (X or Z stabilizer)
    ─□───●───□───●───□─
     │   │   │   │   │         distance d lattice:
    ─●───□───●───□───●─          2d² − 1 physical qubits
     │   │   │   │   │           corrects ⌊(d−1)/2⌋ errors
    ─□───●───□───●───□─

Below a threshold physical error rate p_th ≈ 10⁻³, logical error is suppressed exponentially in code distance:

    p_logical ≈ (p_physical / p_th)^{(d+1)/2}

The threshold is the crucial concept. Above it, adding qubits makes things worse. Below it, every increase in d buys exponential improvement for polynomial cost. Current hardware has crossed the threshold — Google's 2023 and 2024 results demonstrated that a distance-5 logical qubit outperforms distance-3, confirming the scaling works in practice.

The overhead

  p_physical   d needed for p_L = 10⁻¹⁵   physical qubits per logical
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    10⁻³              ~27                        ~1,500
    10⁻⁴              ~13                          ~340

Add magic-state distillation for T gates — each logical T consumes many distilled states, and distillation factories can occupy the majority of a device's qubits.

RSA-2048 in these terms: ~4,100 logical qubits × ~5,000 physical each ≈ 2 × 10⁷ physical qubits, running ~8 hours. Against ~10³ today. The gap is four orders of magnitude, and it is overhead rather than physics — which is why forecasts vary so widely.

Honest assessment

Demonstrated:

  • Quantum error correction below threshold, with logical error decreasing as distance grows.
  • Random circuit sampling beyond practical classical simulation (Google 2019, USTC 2020–21) — though classical algorithms have repeatedly narrowed these claims, which is itself informative.
  • Small-scale chemistry simulation, useful as validation rather than as new science.

Not demonstrated:

  • Any commercially valuable computation faster than the best classical method.
  • Factoring a number that classical methods could not factor instantly.
  • A variational algorithm outperforming classical optimization on a real problem.

Where advantage is most credible, in order: quantum simulation of quantum systems (Feynman's original argument, and the only case where the mapping is native); then cryptanalysis, once fault tolerance arrives; then optimization, where claims remain unsubstantiated and the shot-count economics of Week 9 are frequently omitted.

Analogy boundary — NISQ as early classical computing. The comparison to 1950s mainframes suggests steady scaling toward general usefulness. It misleads in both directions. Worse: without error correction, NISQ devices cannot simply be scaled — noise compounds exponentially, so a bigger noisy device is not a better one, and there is no Moore's-law path from here to there. Better: the theoretical foundation is far more developed than in 1950. We know what fault tolerance requires, we have proofs of the algorithms, and the threshold has been crossed experimentally. The remaining work is engineering at scale, which is a different kind of hard than "we do not know if this is possible."

Ethics and near-term posture

Cryptographic migration is the concrete near-term obligation, and it is not speculative. Harvest-now-decrypt-later means data requiring 30-year confidentiality needs post-quantum protection today. NIST's standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) were finalized in 2024; the engineering question is crypto-agility — whether your systems can swap primitives without redesign.

Access and concentration. Quantum computing requires capital at a scale that concentrates capability in a few states and firms. Cloud access mitigates this partially and creates its own dependency.

Overclaiming. The field has a persistent gap between marketing and results. As someone who will be asked to evaluate these claims professionally, the questions worth asking: Logical or physical qubits? What is the two-qubit gate fidelity? Was the classical baseline the best known algorithm or a convenient one? Was the circuit compiled with knowledge of the answer?

Key takeaways

  • QEC works by measuring stabilizers — error syndromes — without measuring the logical state, circumventing no-cloning and measurement collapse.
  • Syndrome measurement digitizes continuous errors, which is why a finite correction set suffices.
  • Below the ~10⁻³ threshold, logical error falls exponentially in code distance. Hardware has crossed this threshold.
  • Overhead is the barrier: ~10³–10⁴ physical qubits per logical qubit, putting RSA-2048 at ~10⁷ physical qubits against ~10³ today.
  • No commercially valuable quantum advantage has been demonstrated. Cryptographic migration is nonetheless a present-tense obligation.

Q1 (Objective 8) — Explain how syndrome measurement corrects an error of arbitrary continuous angle using only discrete corrections, and why this does not violate the continuity of quantum evolution.

Q2 (Objective 8) — A vendor announces 1,000 qubits and claims imminent RSA-2048 factoring. Write the assessment you would give a CISO, with the specific questions that determine whether the claim is credible.

Check your answers

A1 — Consider a small rotation error on one qubit of |ψ⟩_L = α|000⟩ + β|111⟩:

    E = cos θ · I + i sin θ · X₀
    E|ψ⟩_L = cos θ · |ψ⟩_L  +  i sin θ · X₀|ψ⟩_L

The state is now a superposition of "no error" and "bit flip on qubit 0." The error is continuous — θ can take any value.

Now measure the stabilizers Z₀Z₁ and Z₁Z₂. The no-error component has syndrome (+1, +1); the flipped component has (−1, +1). These are orthogonal eigenspaces, so the measurement projects onto one:

    outcome (+1,+1) with probability cos²θ  →  state is exactly |ψ⟩_L, no correction needed
    outcome (−1,+1) with probability sin²θ  →  state is exactly X₀|ψ⟩_L, apply X₀ to fix

Either way the post-measurement state is exactly correct — no residual error of size θ remains. The continuous parameter has been converted into a discrete outcome plus a probability.

This does not violate continuity of evolution because measurement is not unitary evolution. The unitary part of the dynamics remains perfectly continuous; the projection is the non-unitary step, and it is the same projection postulate from Week 10 used constructively. The deep point: the set of correctable errors need only span the space of possible errors, not enumerate them. Correcting {I, X, Y, Z} on each qubit suffices to correct any single-qubit error, because those four operators form a basis for all 2×2 matrices.

A2 — Assessment: not credible on the stated facts, and no change to migration planning.

The claim fails on arithmetic before any evaluation of the vendor. RSA-2048 requires ~4,100 logical qubits. At current physical error rates, each logical qubit costs ~10³–10⁴ physical qubits under the surface code, giving ~2 × 10⁷ physical qubits. A 1,000-qubit device is four orders of magnitude short. If the 1,000 are physical — which they invariably are — the device supports at most a handful of logical qubits, and plausibly none at useful distance.

Questions that determine credibility:

  1. Logical or physical qubits? If unstated, assume physical. A specific logical-qubit count at a stated code distance and logical error rate is the only meaningful figure.
  2. Two-qubit gate fidelity and T2? Below-threshold error (< 10⁻³) is necessary for any fault-tolerance claim. Ask for randomized benchmarking numbers, not marketing figures.
  3. What has actually been factored, and how? If the answer is 15, 21, or 35, ask whether the modular exponentiation circuit was general or compiled with knowledge of the factors — the 2013 Smolin result showed most such demonstrations reduce to trivial circuits.
  4. Is there a published resource estimate? Peer-reviewed estimates (Gidney–Ekerå 2021 is the standard reference) put RSA-2048 at ~20 million physical qubits and 8 hours.
  5. What is the classical baseline? Compare against the best known classical algorithm, not a convenient one.

Recommendation regardless of this vendor's claim: proceed with post-quantum migration on the existing schedule. The justification is harvest-now-decrypt-later, not any particular announcement — traffic captured today is decryptable whenever a capable machine appears, so the migration deadline is set by your data's confidentiality lifetime, not by the hardware roadmap. Prioritize long-lived signatures and archival confidentiality; treat crypto-agility as the durable requirement, since it is the only property that survives being wrong about timing in either direction.
