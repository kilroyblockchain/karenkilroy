The Bet Einstein Lost

Superposition, entanglement, and the 1935 argument between Einstein and quantum mechanics that took 29 years to become a testable question, 37 to become a real experiment — and 87 to win a Nobel Prize.

This reading covers Objectives 1–3: the physical foundations of superposition and entanglement, the historical argument (EPR) about whether quantum mechanics is even complete, and the experiment (Bell's theorem) that finally settled it. These are the two ideas — superposition and entanglement — that everything else in this course, from qubits to Grover's algorithm, is built on top of.

01 — Superposition

Holding Every Bet at Once

A regular computer bit is always definitely 0 or definitely 1 — like a light switch that's either on or off, no in-between. A qubit doesn't have to make that choice until it's measured. Before measurement, it can exist in a superposition: a genuine blend of both 0 and 1 at once, not "secretly one or the other and we just don't know which."

Analogy — the parlay you haven't settled yet

Think of a superposition like a same-game parlay that hasn't resolved yet. It's not that the parlay secretly already won or lost and you just haven't checked the score — while the games are still live, the bet genuinely exists in a mixed, unresolved state, leaning toward one outcome or another based on the odds. The moment the final whistle blows (the "measurement"), it snaps to one definite result: win or lose, nothing in between.

Math refresher — before this gets equation-heavy

You'll see superposition written like this:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

Don't let the symbols intimidate you — |0⟩ and |1⟩ are just labels for "the qubit reads 0" and "the qubit reads 1" (that bracket notation is called Dirac notation — it's just a container for a state, nothing more exotic than that). α and β are numbers that tell you how much of each outcome is "in the mix." The rule is: take the size of α and the size of β, square each one, and they must add up to 1 — because when you finally measure, you have to land on something. (The bars in |α|² mean "size of," which matters because these numbers can carry a direction as well as a magnitude — more on that later in the course.) |α|² is literally the probability of measuring 0, and |β|² is the probability of measuring 1.

Analogy — the mixing board

If you've ever used a music mixing board or a DJ crossfader, think of α and β as two channel volumes blended together into one signal. Turn one channel up and the other down, and you get a different mix — but until you "print" the final track (measure it), both channels are genuinely present in the sound, not just one of them hiding behind the other.

In quantum computing, this is what gives quantum computers their potential power: a single qubit holds a blend of 2 states, but n qubits together can hold a superposition of 2ⁿ states simultaneously. A modest 20-qubit register can represent over a million states at once. The catch — and this matters, because it's the single most common misconception about quantum computing — is that measuring still only ever gives you one definite answer. The real engineering challenge (which you'll see later in Grover's and Shor's algorithms) is designing a computation so that the useful answer becomes overwhelmingly likely by the time you measure, through an effect called interference.

Simplification note: the deeper mechanics of how interference amplifies the "right" answer involves complex-valued amplitudes and phase — that's coming later in the course once you've built up more of the mathematical toolkit. For now, the conceptual picture (many states blended, one state observed) is the correct foundation.

See it move: an interactive qubit

02 — Entanglement

Linked Fates

Entanglement happens when two or more qubits become correlated in a way that can't be broken down into "qubit A is doing its own thing, and qubit B is doing its own separate thing." Once entangled, they behave as one combined system — measuring one instantly tells you something about the other, no matter how far apart they are.

Analogy — hedged positions on opposite sides of the world

Picture two trading desks, one in New York and one in Tokyo, holding perfectly hedged positions on the same underlying asset — structured so that the instant one desk's position resolves, you know exactly what the other desk's position resolved to, even though no phone call happened between them. Entanglement has that flavor: correlated outcomes without any signal being sent. Where the analogy breaks down is that in finance, both positions were secretly determined the whole time — with entanglement, neither qubit had a definite value until the first measurement happened.

Analogy — the linked controller

In gaming terms, it's less like two players secretly following the same walkthrough (that would just be a hidden variable — see the next section) and more like two controllers that are, at a deep level, one input device pretending to be two. Pressing a button on one doesn't "send a signal" to the other — but the game engine treats their outputs as a single correlated event.

Math refresher — the entangled state

A common entangled two-qubit state (called a Bell state) is written:

|ψ⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2

Read this as: there's a 50% chance both qubits measure 0, and a 50% chance both measure 1 — but a 0% chance of getting one of each. Critically, you cannot rewrite this as "qubit A's state" times "qubit B's state" separately — the correlation is baked into the combined description itself, not into each qubit individually. That inseparability is the formal definition of entanglement.

Entanglement isn't just a curiosity — it's a genuine computational resource. It's essential to quantum teleportation, superdense coding, and quantum error correction, and it's one of the two core ingredients (along with superposition) behind the computational advantages quantum computers can have over classical ones. But in 1935, entanglement's implications looked less like an engineering resource and more like a threat to physics as Einstein understood it — which is exactly what the EPR paper was about.

03 — The EPR Paradox

Einstein Calls Foul

In 1935, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen (EPR) published a paper arguing that quantum mechanics, despite its experimental success, could not be a complete description of physical reality.

Their argument used an entangled pair of particles. If you measure one particle's position, you instantly know the other's position. If you instead measure the first particle's momentum, you instantly know the other's momentum. Since you could have chosen either measurement, and the second particle was never touched, EPR argued that both position and momentum must have already been definite, "real" properties of the second particle all along — before anyone measured anything.

Analogy — the receipt in the sealed envelope

EPR's intuition was basically: if I mail you a sealed envelope and, without opening it, I can correctly predict what's inside just by looking at my copy of the receipt, then surely there was always a definite answer inside that envelope — it's absurd to say the contents only became real the moment someone opened it. That's a completely reasonable way to think about receipts and envelopes. EPR's claim was that particles should work the same way.

The problem: standard quantum mechanics says a particle cannot simultaneously have a well-defined position and momentum — that's Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. So EPR were pointing at a real tension: either quantum mechanics is incomplete (there are hidden, pre-existing properties it fails to describe — often called hidden variables), or nature genuinely allows this "spooky action at a distance," which Einstein flatly refused to accept.

This was controversial in 1935 because it directly challenged the Copenhagen interpretation's claim to be the full picture of reality. It's still discussed today because EPR sits at the exact center of the modern debate over what entanglement actually implies about the universe — and because, for 29 years, it remained a purely philosophical argument. There was no experiment anyone could point to that would settle it either way. That changed in 1964.

04 — Bell's Theoremthe required deep dive

The Experiment That Settled It

John Stewart Bell, an Irish physicist at CERN, did something in 1964 that turned a 29-year-old philosophy debate into a testable science question. He didn't try to guess whether EPR or Bohr was "right." Instead, he asked: if EPR is right — if particles carry pre-existing, definite hidden properties, and nothing travels faster than light — what statistical pattern would that force on the data?

The game, not the math

Think of it as a game show

Imagine a game show where you and a partner are separated into soundproof booths (no communicating once the round starts). A host gives each of you one of a few possible questions, chosen randomly and independently. You each answer yes or no. You win a point when your answers match in specific combinations of questions. If you and your partner agreed on a fixed strategy beforehand — a script, a shared playbook, "hidden variables" you both carry into your booths — there's a hard mathematical ceiling on how often you can win, no matter how clever your strategy is. That ceiling is Bell's inequality.

Math refresher — the inequality, in plain terms

Bell derived that any "pre-agreed strategy" theory (a local hidden-variable theory) must satisfy a hard limit on how strongly the two sides' results can correlate. The version almost everyone quotes — and the one experiments actually test — was sharpened five years later by Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, so it is usually called the CHSH inequality:

S ≤ 2

where S is a combined score built from correlations measured at several different detector-angle combinations. You don't need to derive this yourself — the key fact is that 2 is the hard ceiling for any classical, pre-scripted strategy. Quantum mechanics predicts you can do better than that ceiling using entangled qubits instead of a pre-agreed script — up to S = 2√2 ≈ 2.83. That's not a small statistical wobble; it's a completely different regime, and it's why the test is decisive rather than ambiguous.

This was the breakthrough: Bell's inequality gave physicists an actual number to go measure in a lab. If real entangled particles broke through the classical ceiling of 2, local hidden-variable theories — the entire EPR picture — would be dead. If they never exceeded it, Einstein would have been right all along.

The experimental setup

Why the angles must be random — and chosen late

That one sentence in the caption is doing enormous work, and it is the reason Bell tests took fifty years to get right. If the two detector angles were fixed in advance, or picked in any way the particles could have "known about" when they left the source, then a clever pre-agreed script could reproduce the quantum-looking numbers. The whole experiment would prove nothing.

Analogy — the patched speedrun

Think of a speedrunner who has memorised a level frame by frame. Their run looks superhuman, but only because the level is identical every time. The way you find out whether they are genuinely reacting or just replaying a memorised script is to randomise the level after they have started running. A Bell test does exactly this to the particles: it decides which question to ask each detector so late that no memorised script — and no message travelling at light speed from the other detector — could have accounted for it.

So a convincing Bell test has to satisfy three conditions at once: the two choices must be independent of each other, independent of whatever the source did, and made late enough and far enough apart that light itself could not carry one choice to the other side before both measurements finish. Each of those conditions, left open, is a way for local realism to survive — and each one has its own name.

The three escape routes

Physicists call these loopholes. None of them is a flaw in Bell's reasoning; each is a practical gap between the ideal experiment Bell described and the messy one you can actually build. Closing them, one at a time and then all at once, is the story of the last fifty years:

LoopholeThe escape it leaves openHow it was closed
Detection
(“fair sampling”)		 Early detectors caught only a tiny fraction of photons. If the ones that got detected weren't a representative sample, the correlation could be an artefact of which pairs you happened to see. Use systems you can read out almost every time — trapped ions (Rowe, 2001) or high-efficiency photon detectors (2013 onward).
Locality
(“communication”)		 If the detectors are close together or slow to choose, one side could in principle influence the other at or below light speed without breaking any physics. Push the detectors apart and switch the settings while the particles are in flight — Aspect (1982), then Weihs (1998) at 400 m.
Freedom of choice Subtler: what if the “random” setting choices share some common cause with the particles themselves? Then the choices were never really free. Seed the choices from something wildly independent — light from quasars emitted billions of years ago (Rauch, 2018), or random bits typed by over 100,000 people (the BIG Bell Test, 2018).

Until 2015, every experiment closed some of these while leaving another open, which left just enough room for a determined sceptic. That year, three independent groups — in Delft, Vienna, and Boulder — closed the detection and locality loopholes simultaneously for the first time. The Delft experiment separated its two entangled electrons, held in defects in diamond, by 1.3 kilometres.

The experimental chase to actually run this test took decades, and the measured numbers are worth seeing rather than just being told about:

Notice that the 2015 numbers are lower than Aspect's 1982 number, which looks backwards until you see why. A bigger S is not a better experiment. The loophole-free setups traded raw correlation strength for airtightness — harder physical systems, longer distances, stricter timing — so their S sits closer to 2 while ruling out far more. What matters is that the violation survives once the escape routes are shut.

Across every rigorous repetition, the measured correlations break through the classical limit of 2. The conclusion physicists draw from this is significant: nature does not behave according to local realism — the combined idea that particles carry definite pre-existing properties (realism) and that no influence travels faster than light between them (locality). At least one of those two assumptions has to go, and most physicists conclude it's realism in the EPR sense: entangled particles genuinely don't have definite values until measured.

Simplification note: "nothing travels faster than light" is preserved — no usable information is actually transmitted between the two measurements, so this doesn't violate relativity. What's given up is the classical assumption that particles carry fixed, definite properties all along, independent of measurement.

Key takeaways

  • Superposition (α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) lets a qubit hold a genuine blend of 0 and 1 until measured; n qubits can hold 2ⁿ states in superposition at once, though measurement always yields one definite outcome.
  • Entanglement links two or more qubits so their combined state can't be split into separate individual descriptions — measuring one instantly correlates with the other, without transmitting any usable signal.
  • The 1935 EPR paper argued quantum mechanics must be incomplete, since entangled particles seemed to imply pre-existing "hidden variable" properties that quantum theory doesn't describe.
  • In 1964, John Bell derived a testable inequality that turned the EPR philosophical debate into an experimental question; the form experiments actually use (S ≤ 2 for any local hidden-variable theory) is the 1969 CHSH version.
  • Bell tests from Freedman & Clauser (1972) through Aspect (S = 2.697 ± 0.015 in 1982) to the loophole-free experiments of 2015 have consistently violated the classical limit. Closing the detection, locality, and freedom-of-choice loopholes is what made the result decisive — work recognized with the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.

1. Superposition

Superposition is the principle that a quantum system can exist in a combination of multiple possible states at once, rather than being confined to just one, until measured. A qubit can be in a state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, where the coefficients determine the probability of measuring each outcome. In quantum computing, n qubits in superposition can represent 2ⁿ states simultaneously, which is the basis for potential speedups in algorithms like Grover's and Shor's — though a measurement still only yields one definite outcome, so harnessing the parallelism requires carefully designed interference.

2. Entanglement

Entanglement is a phenomenon where two or more particles become correlated such that the group's quantum state cannot be described as the combination of separate individual states. Measuring one entangled particle instantly determines information about its partner's state, regardless of distance, though no information is transmitted faster than light. It's a key computational resource enabling quantum teleportation, superdense coding, and error correction, and is essential (alongside superposition) to quantum computational advantage.

3. The EPR paper

Published in 1935 by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, it argued quantum mechanics must be incomplete. Using entangled particles, they reasoned that measuring either position or momentum on one particle let you instantly know the corresponding property of the other, implying both properties were already definite "elements of reality" — even though quantum mechanics says they can't both be well-defined at once. It was controversial because it directly challenged the Copenhagen interpretation's completeness, and remains controversial today because it's central to ongoing debates about locality, realism, and what entanglement implies about the universe.

4. John Bell's contributions

Bell transformed the EPR debate from philosophy into an experimentally testable question. In his 1964 paper, he derived an inequality that any local hidden-variable theory must satisfy, and showed that quantum mechanics predicts violations of it under certain conditions — meaning experiments could definitively distinguish between the two pictures of reality.

5. Bell's inequality and experimental results

Bell's inequality sets an upper limit on correlations between entangled-particle measurements that any local hidden-variable theory could produce. Quantum mechanics predicts correlations exceeding this limit under specific setups. Starting with Clauser and Freedman's 1970s experiments, followed by Aspect's more rigorous 1980s tests, and later loophole-closing experiments by Zeilinger's team and others in the 2000s–2010s, physicists have repeatedly measured violations matching quantum mechanics' predictions rather than the classical limit — work recognized with the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.
