When certainty broke: the birth of quantum mechanics

Two young physicists, working almost a year apart and from opposite starting points, independently invented the mathematics of the quantum world — and then had to argue with Einstein about what that mathematics actually meant.

Week 3 reading ~30 min Objective 1: the historical evolution of physics from Newtonian mechanics into early quantum theory

This week you're going to meet the two people most responsible for quantum mechanics as we now teach it, watch them clash with Einstein at possibly the most famous conference photo ever taken, and land on the interpretation of quantum theory that most textbooks — including this course — still teach first: the Copenhagen interpretation. None of this is trivia. The measurement problem you'll meet at the end of this reading is the same conceptual gap that later ideas like superposition and entanglement (objective 2, coming soon) are built to formalize. Understanding why physicists were so unsettled in 1927 makes the strangeness of qubits feel a lot less arbitrary later on.

Quick vector refresher

A couple of sections below use the idea of a state as an arrow — something with both a length and a direction, like a joystick pushed partway toward a corner of the screen. In classical physics, an arrow like this might represent velocity: 30 mph, heading northeast. In quantum mechanics, we'll use a similar arrow — called a state vector — to represent everything that can currently be said about a particle's condition. You don't need any calculus for this reading; just the idea that "pointing in a certain direction" can stand in for "being in a certain condition."

1The birth of quantum mechanics

By 1900, physics looked close to finished. Newton's laws predicted the motion of planets and cannonballs alike; Maxwell's equations explained light and electricity. Then a small number of experiments refused to fit. Hot glowing objects radiated energy in a pattern that classical physics said was impossible. Light hitting a metal plate knocked electrons loose in a way that made no sense if light was purely a wave. Atoms, according to the accepted rules of electromagnetism, should have collapsed in a fraction of a second — and yet atoms were obviously stable.

Max Planck's 1900 proposal that energy comes in discrete chunks, or quanta, was meant as a mathematical patch, not a revolution — he didn't fully believe it himself at first. Einstein took it seriously in 1905 to explain the photoelectric effect, and Niels Bohr used it in 1913 to build a model of the atom where electrons could only occupy certain fixed orbits. Each of these was a brilliant patch on top of classical physics rather than a new theory in its own right. What quantum mechanics needed by the mid-1920s was a complete, self-consistent mathematical framework — not more patches. That framework arrived from two directions at once, within about a year of each other.

  1. 1900
    Planck introduces energy quanta to explain glowing-hot objects.
  2. 1905
    Einstein explains the photoelectric effect using light quanta (photons).
  3. 1913
    Bohr proposes an atom with fixed, quantized electron orbits.
  4. 1925

  5. 1926

Six milestones from the first crack in classical physics to the framework physicists still use today.

2Heisenberg's breakthrough

Werner Heisenberg was 23 in 1925, recovering from hay fever on the remote island of Helgoland, when he had the key insight: stop trying to picture what an electron is doing inside the atom, and only work with what can actually be measured — the frequencies and intensities of light the atom emits.

Sports analogy

Imagine a coach who never watches game footage and refuses to picture a player's exact position on the field at any instant. Instead, the coach only works from the box score: shots taken, passes completed, goals scored. It sounds like a strange restriction, but the box score is real — it's exactly what you can actually observe and measure, game after game. Heisenberg did the equivalent for the atom: instead of imagining an electron tracing a smooth orbit (something nobody can observe), he organized only the observable quantities — the "box score" of spectral lines — into a grid of numbers. That grid is a matrix, and the resulting theory is called matrix mechanics. Max Born and Pascual Jordan quickly showed Heisenberg's scheme was fully consistent mathematics, and by late 1925 it was the first complete, internally consistent version of quantum theory.

Two years later, in 1927, Heisenberg made an even more famous contribution: the uncertainty principle. It states that there is a hard mathematical limit on how precisely you can simultaneously know certain pairs of properties — position and momentum, for instance. This is not a statement about clumsy instruments that might one day be improved. It is a built-in feature of nature: the more precisely a particle's position is pinned down, the less precisely its momentum can be known, no matter how good your equipment gets. This was a genuinely new kind of claim in physics — not "we don't know yet," but "this cannot be known, even in principle."

3Schrödinger's breakthrough

Erwin Schrödinger disliked Heisenberg's approach — he found working with abstract grids of numbers, with no picture to visualize, deeply unsatisfying. Building on Louis de Broglie's idea that particles have a wave-like nature, Schrödinger spent the winter of 1925–26 developing an alternative: describe the electron not as a particle in an orbit, but as a wave spread through space.

Music analogy

Pluck a guitar string and it doesn't vibrate randomly — it settles into specific allowed patterns, or modes: the fundamental note, then the octave above it, then the fifth above that, and so on. Each mode has a fixed shape and a fixed frequency; you can't get a note "in between" two adjacent modes on an open string. Schrödinger's equation treats an electron bound to an atom the same way: instead of a string vibrating in one dimension, you get a three-dimensional wave — the wave function, written ψ (the Greek letter psi) — that can only settle into specific allowed patterns around the nucleus. Each allowed pattern corresponds to a specific, quantized energy level, in the same way each mode of the guitar string corresponds to a specific note. The "weirdness" of quantized atomic energy levels turns out to be the same mathematics as why a guitar string can't play a note halfway between two frets.

The Schrödinger equation describes how this wave function evolves smoothly over time — no jumps, no grids, just a continuous wave, which felt far more intuitive to most physicists of the day. Remarkably, within months it was proven that Schrödinger's wave mechanics and Heisenberg's matrix mechanics, despite looking completely different, make identical predictions. They are two languages for the same underlying theory. Today we mostly use Schrödinger's picture for its visual intuition and Heisenberg's for certain calculations — but in 1926, that equivalence wasn't yet clear, and each camp initially believed the other was on the wrong track.

4The 1927 Solvay Conference

In October 1927, the Fifth Solvay Conference on Electrons and Photons brought 29 of the era's leading physicists to Brussels to argue out what this new mathematics actually meant. Seventeen of the attendees either had already won, or would go on to win, a Nobel Prize. Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Marie Curie, Max Born, Paul Dirac, Louis de Broglie, and Wolfgang Pauli were all in the room.

The conference is remembered less for its official talks and more for what happened in the hallways and over dinner: the start of the running argument between Einstein and Bohr, now called the Bohr–Einstein debates. Einstein was uneasy with the probabilistic, indeterminate picture that Heisenberg and Bohr were proposing — this is the origin of his later remark that "God does not play dice." Over breakfast each morning at the conference, Einstein would present a thought experiment designed to show quantum mechanics was incomplete or inconsistent; by dinner, Bohr would usually have found the flaw in it. Neither man ever fully persuaded the other, but the exchange sharpened the emerging interpretation into something the rest of the physics community could evaluate — and largely accept.

5The Copenhagen interpretation and the measurement problem

The view that Bohr and Heisenberg defended at Solvay, developed over the previous two years at Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, holds a few core claims:

  • A quantum system does not have a definite value for a property — like position — until that property is actually measured.
  • Before measurement, the system exists in a superposition: a combination of every possible outcome, weighted by probability.
  • The probabilities are governed by the Born rule, using the wave function ψ.
  • The act of measurement causes the wave function to collapse into one definite, actual outcome.

This collapse is the heart of what's called the measurement problem: the mathematics of the Schrödinger equation describes smooth, continuous evolution with no collapse in it anywhere — so what, physically, is actually happening at the moment of measurement, and why does looking at something seem to change it? Bohr's answer, roughly, was not to ask. He argued that quantum and classical descriptions apply to different domains — the quantum system being measured, and the classical measuring device doing the measuring — and that physics only needs to predict outcomes, not settle what's "really" happening in between. Not everyone found (or finds) that satisfying, which is exactly why alternative interpretations exist, covered in the next section.

Gaming analogy

Think of a multiplayer game with a fog-of-war map. Until you actually move a unit into an unexplored tile, the game engine doesn't fully commit to what's sitting there — it might be tracking several possibilities at once for efficiency. The moment your scout's line of sight reaches that tile, the game "resolves" it into one definite thing. Copenhagen's picture of measurement has a similar flavor: the electron isn't hiding a definite position that you simply haven't found yet — in this interpretation, it doesn't have one until the measurement happens. That's a much stronger and stranger claim than "we don't know where it is," and it's the part of quantum mechanics most people find hardest to accept.

6Other interpretations

Copenhagen remains the interpretation most often taught first, but it never fully settled the argument it started at Solvay in 1927. Because every interpretation below makes the exact same experimental predictions, choosing between them is more a matter of philosophical taste and mathematical elegance than something a lab experiment can currently decide.

InterpretationCore ideaAt measurementDeterministic?
CopenhagenNo definite property until measured; only probabilities exist beforehand.Wave function collapses to one outcome.No — outcomes are fundamentally random.
Many-worldsEvery possible outcome happens, each in its own branching universe.No collapse — the universe splits.Yes, for the whole multiverse; random from inside any one branch.
Pilot-wave (de Broglie–Bohm)Particles always have a real, definite position, guided by a hidden wave.Reveals a position that was already real, just unknown.Yes — but the guiding wave is nonlocal.
Objective collapseCollapse is a real, spontaneous physical process, not caused by observers.Genuine physical collapse, built into the equations.No — collapse is random, like radioactive decay.

A few others worth knowing by name: QBism treats the wave function as a description of one observer's personal beliefs rather than an objective feature of the world, and consistent histories analyzes quantum systems in terms of which sequences of events can be talked about together without contradiction. None of these interpretations changes a single prediction of quantum mechanics — they change what story you tell about why the predictions come out the way they do.

8Key takeaways

  • Heisenberg's matrix mechanics (1925) built quantum theory purely from measurable quantities; his 1927 uncertainty principle showed some pairs of properties can never both be known precisely, even in principle.
  • Schrödinger's wave mechanics (1926) described particles as waves obeying a smooth equation, and was later proven mathematically equivalent to Heisenberg's approach.
  • The 1927 Solvay Conference is remembered for the start of the Bohr–Einstein debates, where Einstein pushed back on the new theory's reliance on probability.
  • The Copenhagen interpretation says properties don't have definite values until measured, and that measurement collapses a superposition into one outcome — this is the measurement problem.
  • Many-worlds, pilot-wave theory, objective collapse, and other interpretations all match every experiment Copenhagen matches — they differ only in the story behind the same math.

Objective 1 · intro

4. What is the Copenhagen interpretation?

Need a hint?
What does it claim happens to a property — like position — before you measure it?
Check your answer
The Copenhagen interpretation, developed mainly by Bohr and Heisenberg in the late 1920s, holds that a quantum system doesn't have definite properties until measured; instead it exists in a superposition described by the wave function, with probabilities given by the Born rule. On measurement, the wave function "collapses" to one definite outcome. It also includes complementarity — that particles can behave as waves or particles depending on the experiment, but never show both at once — and treats quantum randomness as fundamental, not a gap in our knowledge.
Objective 1 · stretch, optional

5. What are other interpretations of quantum mechanics?

Need a hint?
There are several — think about worlds splitting apart, a hidden wave that guides a real particle, and a collapse that happens on its own.
Check your answer
Besides Copenhagen, several alternatives exist: many-worlds (every outcome actually happens, each in a separate branching universe, so no collapse is needed); pilot-wave theory (particles always have a real position guided by a hidden wave, restoring determinism); objective collapse theories (collapse is a real, spontaneous physical process); QBism (the wave function reflects one observer's beliefs, not an objective fact); and consistent histories (analyzing which sequences of events can be described together without contradiction). All make identical experimental predictions to Copenhagen — they differ in what they say is "really" happening underneath.
